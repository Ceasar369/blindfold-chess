# Chapter 4 — Position memory

A still position, held in my head: every piece on its square, from one look, one reading, or a name I know by heart.

A level is one step of a challenge's difficulty: it sets each dial, such as how many pieces stand on the board or how the position reaches me, to an exact value.

A round is one sitting at one level: a fixed number of questions, ending on my score, or sooner when its minutes run out.

## What I can do at the end

- I name what stands on any square of the starting position, with no piece on the board. Every blindfold game starts there.
- I rebuild each of 9 named openings from its name alone. When a blindfold game reaches one, I hold its 28 to 30 pieces besides the kings as the starting position and a few changes.
- I rebuild a real position of 10 pieces besides the kings, after one look. A blindfold endgame asks me to hold every piece at once.
- I name what stands on any square of a real position of 7 pieces besides the kings, read once in words, with no board. Before each move in a blindfold game, I ask what stands where my piece would land.
- I rebuild, whole and with no board, a real position of 7 pieces besides the kings, seen once on a board. Placing every piece myself, on a board I reveal, checks what I hold.

Unproven: that these drills make my blindfold play better. Recall of real positions tracked rating in 51 novices: a link, not a test of training (my references, `drafts-and-science/science/astudillo-sandoval-2026-novice-chess-cognition.md`). Two novices trained over fifteen sessions went from about 15% to 40-50% of game positions recalled, and nothing measured their play (`existing-courses-and-drills/gobet-jackson-2001-novice-memory-training.md`).

## What it needs first

These levels of chapters 1 and 2:

- 1.1.1 L2 — finding a square on a board with no names.
- 1.1.1 L3 — the same within 2 seconds. From 4.1.1 L4, a square is only named, and I find it at once in the position I hold.
- 2.2.1 L4 — a position given in words, which stays on screen there. Here the words hide.

Each card names the levels it needs. No level needs chapter 2 finished, or any of chapter 3.

## The sections

**4.1 — What stands there.** One square at a time, from a position held in my head. 4.1.1 Name what stands there.

**4.2 — Whole positions.** A position I have never seen, held whole once it hides. 4.2.1 Rebuild the position.

**4.3 — Openings by heart.** Named opening positions, held as the starting position and what moved. Nine openings, one challenge each: 4.3.1 Italian Game, 4.3.2 Ruy Lopez, 4.3.3 Scotch Game, 4.3.4 Queen's Gambit, 4.3.5 London System, 4.3.6 Sicilian Defense, 4.3.7 French Defense, 4.3.8 Caro-Kann Defense, 4.3.9 Slav Defense.

**Three answers.** Rebuilding a position on a board and with no board give one answer, the whole position. So they are one challenge, 4.2.1: placed on a board, then, at its last level, on an empty one I reveal. Answering a question about it gives one square's content, so it is its own challenge, 4.1.1: a game asks one square at a time. Each opening of 4.3 also rebuilds a whole position, but a named one I learn for good, never one just seen: at its last level, I am given only the name.

**How a position reaches me.** On a board, then hidden, as chapters 2 and 3 begin on a board. At the levels that read it, in words, then hidden: a blindfold game hands me positions in words and moves. At 4.2.1's last level it is on a board again, then hidden, and no board shows until I reveal an empty one to place on. The words read as chapter 2 writes them: White first, then Black, each side's king first, then its queen, rooks, bishops, knights and pawns, each kind's squares from the a-file on. "White: king h2, rook f5, pawn h3. Black: king g7, rook b1, pawn h6."

**The look.** I look as long as I like, then hide the position myself. No level caps it: a game never shows me the board, so how fast I take in a picture is not what it asks. Other trainers cap it: 10 seconds for 6 to 12 pieces, then 8 (`existing-courses-and-drills/thememorychess-memory-ladder-guide.md`), or 2.5 seconds a piece (`github-trainers/pages/jego-m--chess-memory-game.md`). In earlier studies, more time helped the weaker players, who still stayed behind (`drafts-and-science/science/campitelli-gobet-2005-minds-eye-blindfold.md`, read second-hand, on players far above 1000). My look time is counted, and the pass marks read it. Once hidden, the position never shows again until I answer.

**The wait.** No level adds a wait between the hide and the answer. The five questions, or the rebuild itself, already make me hold the position after it hides. One test waits 30 seconds before its rebuild (`existing-courses-and-drills/de-groot-position-memory-test-blindfoldchess-net.md`).

**No clock.** No level times a question: holding is the skill here, not speed. My average time per answer is counted, and the pass marks read it.

**Real and set-up positions.** Every new position here is real: it stood in a game people played, and a blindfold game only ever holds real ones. Strong players beat weak ones mainly on real positions; on random ones, the gap is much smaller (`drafts-and-science/science/gobet-simon-1996-random-positions-skill.md`, `drafts-and-science/science/sala-gobet-2017-random-material-meta-analysis.md`). Recall of real positions tracked rating even in novices (`astudillo-sandoval-2026-novice-chess-cognition.md`). So real positions train what separates players, and random ones mostly do not. My handoff proposes both kinds, clearly told apart (`02-learning-principles.md`, Proposed); here, set-up positions serve only as below. One trainer's store page also says it takes real positions over random pieces (`existing-courses-and-drills/mindchess.md`).

Real positions come from Lichess's game database (`content-and-progress/content/lichess-standard-games-database.md`): games between players rated 1000 to 1500, of 5 minutes or more each, that ended normally. A position is taken right after the move that first leaves the level's count of pieces besides the kings. My references call positions taken from games the most natural kind (`content-and-progress/content/realistic-random-positions.md`). In January 2013's games alone, 1,016 such games reach 4, 2,049 reach 7, and 3,349 reach 10 (`position-memory-lookups/lichess-games-2013-01-positions.md`). Every example below comes from one of them.

A set-up position serves in one place only: a missed position comes back mirrored, set up from the real one. Mirrored, each piece keeps its rank and moves to the other wing: a to h, b to g, c to f, d to e. A king on g1 goes to b1, a pawn on f2 to c2. It keeps chapter 2's rule for a set-up position: both kings, never side by side, never both in check, no pawn on the first or eighth rank.

**The starting position.** White: king e1, queen d1, rooks a1 and h1, bishops c1 and f1, knights b1 and g1, pawns a2, b2, c2, d2, e2, f2, g2 and h2. Black: king e8, queen d8, rooks a8 and h8, bishops c8 and f8, knights b8 and g8, pawns a7, b7, c7, d7, e7, f7, g7 and h7 (`existing-courses-and-drills/lichess-learn-and-practice.md`, Lichess's lesson on setting up the board; `position-memory-lookups/lichess-games-2013-01-positions.md`). It is asked first, square by square, in 4.1.1 L1: I hold it before this chapter starts. Every opening in 4.3 is built on it.

**Openings.** An opening's position is the starting position and a few changes. In the Italian Game, 5 pieces have moved and 27 stand where they started. 4.3 asks it that way: its board starts in the starting position, and I change only what moved. Experts hold whole boards through templates, shapes stored in memory with places left open (`drafts-and-science/science/gobet-simon-1996-templates-chess-memory.md`). The starting position is the one template every player holds. Unproven that it works this way for a player near 1000.

**What this page leaves out.** Positions changing move by move, except in the first level of each opening in 4.3, which teaches the moves of its line one by one: every other position here stands still. Whose move it is, castling rights and a pawn taken in passing: a position here is only its pieces.

**From my outline's five draft sections** (my handoff, `03-curriculum-outline.md`):

- Small static positions (e.g., 3–4 pieces) — merged into 4.1.1's and 4.2.1's first real levels. A piece count is a dial, not a section. They start at 4 besides the kings, 6 in all, the first rung of one trainer's ladder (`existing-courses-and-drills/thememorychess-memory-ladder-guide.md`).
- Medium static positions (e.g., 6–8 pieces) — merged, as their 7-piece levels.
- Richer positions (e.g., 12–16 pieces) — merged, as 4.2.1 L3: 10 besides the kings, 12 in all, that ladder's top rung. Past it, a player near 1000 holds little from one look: 51 novices rebuilt a median of 4.70 pieces of a real position right (`astudillo-sandoval-2026-novice-chess-cognition.md`), and a player called average rebuilt 9 of 22 in one test (`de-groot-position-memory-test-blindfoldchess-net.md`).
- Full initial setup and familiar opening formations — kept: the starting position in 4.1.1 L1, openings as 4.3.
- Larger/complex positions and full-board reconstruction — merged into 4.3. A full middlegame board from one look is past my reach, by the same two numbers. A full board comes through an opening, held as the starting position and what moved.

**Added: 4.1, questions about a held position.** A game asks one square at a time. Another trainer asks five squares after a look, three holding a piece and two empty (`github-trainers/pages/munznerlouis--chess-viz-trainer.md`). One app's store page says it asks questions about a studied position (`existing-courses-and-drills/eyegambit.md`). In lab studies of strong players, the parts of a position that matter for the move were recalled best (`drafts-and-science/science/saariluoma-kalakoski-1998-apperception-imagery.md`).

## The challenges

### 4.1.1 — Name what stands there

**It trains.** Finding what stands on one square, in a position held in my head.

**It needs.** 1.1.1 L2. From L4, 1.1.1 L3. From L5, 2.2.1 L4.

**What I see.** At L1, no position to look at: it is the starting position, which I hold already. From L2, a real position, on a board with no names on its edge at L2 to L4, or in words at L5, until I hide it. Then each question. At L1 to L3, it shows an empty board, no names on its edge, the square asked lit. At L4 and L5, no board: the square is only named.

**The question.** "What stands on f6?" At L1: "At the start, what stands on c8?"

> **Stand-in.** Whether the question, and at L5 the position in words, is also read aloud. I answer it later, with session 10. Every level plays in silence.

**How I answer.** At every level, I pick one of 13, each a card with its picture: White's king, queen, rook, bishop, knight or pawn, Black's the same, or Empty. White's cards sit on the board's light tone and Black's on its dark. A card never says which is right before I pick. After my pick, it keeps its tile and gains a ring and a badge, a check if right, a cross if wrong; after a wrong pick the right card is ringed too, and the others dim.

**What counts as right.** What stands on the square: its side and its kind, or Empty. No part credit: the right piece on the wrong side is a piece I would take for mine.

**Where its questions come from.**

- L1: the starting position. 16 of its 32 squares holding a piece, and 4 empty squares of ranks 3 to 6, at random, in random order. No square twice in a round.
- From L2: real positions, as the sections say, at the level's count of pieces besides the kings. Each position gives 5 questions: 3 squares holding a piece, at least 1 of them a pawn when it holds one, and 2 empty squares, each next to a piece. So "empty" is never a safe guess for a far corner. One player found pawns the hardest pieces to remember (`drafts-and-science/drafts/chesscom-blog-lpindar-my-first-real-blindfold-game.md`), and another tracks them first (`drafts-and-science/drafts/lichess-forum-any-tips-on-blindfold-chess.md`).

Squares and positions coming back take places among them.

**Levels.**

| Level | The position | Pieces besides the kings | What shows with each question | How I answer | Questions per round | Minutes per round |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| L1 | The starting position, no look | 30 | An empty board, the square lit | Piece picker | 20 | 3 |
| L2 | Real, on a board, until I hide it | 4 | An empty board, the square lit | Piece picker | 20 | 4 |
| L3 | Real, on a board, until I hide it | 7 | An empty board, the square lit | Piece picker | 20 | 5 |
| L4 | Real, on a board, until I hide it | 7 | No board, the square named | Piece picker | 20 | 5 |
| L5 | Real, in words, until I hide them | 7 | No board, the square named | Piece picker | 20 | 5 |

From L2, a round's 20 questions are 4 positions, 5 questions each. Every level offers the 13 choices. They are every answer there is, never near misses. The lit square goes at L4: from there, I find the square in my head, as a game asks.

**Scoring.** One round counts my right answers out of 20, my average time per answer, my average look time from L2, and my mistakes by kind. A question left when the minutes run out is a miss.

> **Stand-in.** Pass mark for 4.1.1 L1 to L5: the right answers in one round, and my average look time. My practice sets it.

**After each answer.**

- Right: "Right: Black's pawn stands on f6."
- Wrong, a piece that stands near the square: "No: Black's pawn stands on f6. White's knight stands on f5, the rank beside."
- Wrong, Empty for a piece: "No: Black's pawn stands on g6. You left it out."
- Wrong, a piece for an empty square: "No: h5 is empty. White's rook stands on h6, the rank beside."

**Mistakes it names.** A wrong answer takes the first kind it fits.

- Chapter 1's five, by where the piece I named stands, against the square asked: I hold that piece one square off. In L4's example position: for f7, Black's king, on g7, the file beside. For g6, Black's king, on g7, the rank beside. For a6, White's pawn, on h3, the board turned. For g6, White's king, on g3, the ranks reversed. In L2's: for h5, White's king, on a5, the files reversed.
- A piece left out: Empty, for g6, in L4's position, where Black's pawn stands. A forgotten piece, one of my handoff's five kinds (`02-learning-principles.md`).
- The wrong side: White's pawn, for f7, in L4's position, where Black's pawn stands.
- The wrong piece: Black's bishop, for d6, in L4's position, where Black's rook stands. A wrong piece, one of my handoff's five.
- A piece where nothing stands: Black's pawn, for e5, in L4's position. e5 is empty, and no Black pawn stands one square off it, by chapter 1's five.
- Another answer: any other wrong one, such as White's queen, for g6.

**What comes back.** At L1, a square I miss comes back once in each of my next two rounds holding this challenge: the starting position is learned as itself. From L2, a position where I miss a question comes back once in each of my next two rounds holding this challenge, mirrored, and asks the mirrored squares. The same position would train that one position. Mirrored, its shape comes back on new squares. L2's example position, mirrored: White: king h5, rook e4, knight c5. Black: king d6, pawns a5 and c6. Its f6 is asked as c6.

**Examples.**

- **L1.** c8 lit on an empty board. "At the start, what stands on c8?" Right: Black's bishop. Wrong: I pick Black's knight, and I am told "No: Black's bishop stands on c8. Black's knight stands on b8, the file beside."
- **L2.** From a Lichess game of January 2013, players rated 1415 and 1500, after White's 46th move, on the board: White: king a5, rook d4, knight f5. Black: king e6, pawns f6 and h5. I hide it, and f6 lights on an empty board. "What stands on f6?" Right: Black's pawn. Wrong: I pick White's knight, and I am told "No: Black's pawn stands on f6. White's knight stands on f5, the rank beside."
- **L3.** From a game between players rated 1413 and 1409, after White's 40th move, on the board: White: king g2, rooks a4 and b6, pawns f2 and g3. Black: king g8, rook b7, pawns a5 and g5. I hide it, and b7 lights. "What stands on b7?" Right: I pick Black's rook. Wrong: I pick White's rook, and I am told "No: Black's rook stands on b7. White's rook stands on b6, the rank beside."
- **L4.** From a game between players rated 1252 and 1322, after Black's 33rd move, on the board: White: king g3, bishop a6, pawns f3 and h3. Black: king g7, rook d6, pawns f7, g6 and h7. I hide it, and no board shows. "What stands on g6?" Right: I pick Black's pawn. Wrong: I pick Empty, and I am told "No: Black's pawn stands on g6. You left it out."
- **L5.** No board. From a game between players rated 1402 and 1322, after Black's 43rd move, in words: "White: king d3, rook h6, pawns c2, g2 and h2. Black: king d6, rook a6, pawns c5 and g6." I hide them. "What stands on h5?" Right: I pick Empty. Wrong: I pick White's rook, and I am told "No: h5 is empty. White's rook stands on h6, the rank beside."

### 4.2.1 — Rebuild the position

**It trains.** Holding a whole position I have never seen, once it hides.

**It needs.** 1.1.1 L2. From L4, 2.2.1 L4.

**What I see.** A real position, on a board with no names on its edge at L1 to L3 and L6, or in words at L4 and L5, until I hide it. At L6 the board stands alone, with no list of its squares beside it. Then, at L1 to L5, an empty board with no names on its edge, to rebuild it on. At L6, no board until I reveal an empty one, and no hint while I answer.

**The question.** "Rebuild this position." It opens with the position: "White: king h2, rook f5, pawn h3. Black: king g7, rook b1, pawn h6."

> **Stand-in.** Whether the position in words, at L4 and L5, is also read aloud. I answer it later, with session 10. Every level plays in silence.

**How I answer.** At L1 to L5, I place each piece on its square, kings included, then confirm. A piece placed wrong can come off before I confirm. At L6, once the board is hidden, I press "Reveal board", then place them the same way.

**What counts as right.** The position, whole: every piece on its square, of its kind and its side, none missing, none added. The order I place them in never counts. No part credit for the position: one piece in the wrong place makes the board in my head wrong. The round also counts each piece right, so a near miss still shows.

**Where its questions come from.** Real positions, as the sections say, at the level's count of pieces besides the kings. Positions coming back take places among them.

**Levels.**

| Level | How the position reaches me | Pieces besides the kings | How I answer | Questions per round | Minutes per round |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| L1 | On a board, until I hide it | 4 | Place them | 5 | 4 |
| L2 | On a board, until I hide it | 7 | Place them | 4 | 5 |
| L3 | On a board, until I hide it | 10 | Place them | 3 | 5 |
| L4 | In words, until I hide them | 4 | Place them | 5 | 5 |
| L5 | In words, until I hide them | 7 | Place them | 4 | 5 |
| L6 | On a board, until I hide it | 7 | Place them, after Reveal board | 3 | 5 |

A question is one position. 4, 7 and 10 besides the kings are 6, 9 and 12 in all: one trainer's ladder runs from 6 to 12 pieces (`existing-courses-and-drills/thememorychess-memory-ladder-guide.md`). Steps of three keep the levels few. Fewer positions as they grow keep a round to 5 minutes. Words start again at 4, since no picture comes with them, and stop at 7, a step past the 6 chapter 2 gave in words, which stayed on screen. L6 comes last, the position back on a board: I hold it whole with no board, until I reveal one to place on.

**Scoring.** One round counts the positions I rebuild whole, and my pieces right, out of every piece in the round, kings included. A piece added where nothing stands, with no piece left out to pair with, takes one more off. A position with one piece wrong is not whole, and gives all its other pieces: 5 of 6 at L1. The round also counts my average look time, my average time per answer, and my mistakes by kind. A question left when the minutes run out is a miss, and its pieces count as left out.

> **Stand-in.** Pass mark for 4.2.1 L1 to L6: the positions rebuilt whole and the pieces right in one round, and my average look time. My practice sets it.

**After each answer.** One board only: my reconstruction, with each square marked. Right: my piece is the right piece there. Wrong: my piece stands there, and another piece belongs there. Extra: my piece stands where nothing belongs. Missing: a piece belongs there and I put none, so the right piece is drawn faint on its square. A square empty in both has no mark. It shows after every answer, right or wrong, at every level: at L4 and L5, where the position was in words, the board shows what I placed, and the words show again beside it. A right answer moves on by itself after a second, and a miss waits for me.

- Right: "Right: all 6 pieces."
- Wrong, a piece on a wrong square: "No: 5 of 6. Black's rook stands on b1, not a1: the file beside."
- Wrong, two pieces swapped: "No: 10 of 12. Black's rook stands on d3, and its bishop on c5: you swapped them."
- Wrong, a piece left out: "No: 8 of 9. You left out Black's pawn on h6."

**Mistakes it names.** One answer can show several. A piece placed where it does not stand pairs with a piece of its kind and side that I left out, and takes the first kind it fits against that piece's square. Two trainers' code labels each square right, wrong piece, missing or added, and a wrong square pairs a missing piece with an added one (`content-and-progress/progress/error-type-tracking-in-chess-trainers.md`).

- Chapter 1's five, against the square the piece belongs on. In L1's example, Black's rook belongs on b1: on a1 or c1, the file beside. On b2, the rank beside. On g8, the board turned. On g1, the files reversed. On b8, the ranks reversed.
- Two pieces swapped: Black's rook on c5 and its bishop on d3, in L3's example, each on the other's square.
- The wrong piece: Black's bishop on d1, in L2's example, where Black's rook stands, the rook left out. A wrong piece, one of my handoff's five kinds (`02-learning-principles.md`).
- The wrong side: White's pawn on f7, in L4's example, where Black's pawn stands.
- A piece left out: Black's pawn on h6, in L5's example. A forgotten piece, one of my handoff's five.
- A piece added: White's pawn on g2, in L1's example, where nothing stands, and nothing is left out.
- Another square: any other wrong one, such as White's rook on c3, for f5, in L1's example.

**What comes back.** A position I miss comes back once in each of my next two rounds at the level I missed it at, mirrored, as the sections say. A round of another level leaves it, and its two rounds, alone: a position of 12 pieces missed at L3 never comes back in L1. At L6 it comes back as L6 shows a position, on a board, then hidden. The same position would train that one position. Mirrored, its shape comes back on new squares. L1's example, mirrored: White: king a2, rook c5, pawn a3. Black: king b7, rook g1, pawn a6.

### 4.3 — The nine openings

Nine challenges, 4.3.1 to 4.3.9, one opening each. They share their levels, their rules and their told lines, and differ in the opening alone. Each opening teaches one fixed position, its checkpoint: the position its line reaches from the starting position, played by the rules of chess. I have no opening memorised yet, so the lesson comes before the test. The three levels of an opening use the same checkpoint. The line is one way to reach it: this page never says an opening has one continuation, and holds no variation, no further opening and no deep theory. Each opening is open on its own, nothing locks, and I can revisit its first level whenever I want.

**It trains.** Knowing the position of a named opening by heart, as the starting position and what moved.

**It needs.** 4.1.1 L1 and 4.2.1 L1, for each of the nine.

**What I see.**

- At L1, the opening's name, and the line played from the starting position: each move on its own, in order, with its departure and its destination marked, an arrow between them, its piece sliding in, and a brief explanation. A move with no line shows only its departure and destination. After each move, I play it myself. Then I play the whole line again from the starting position, with its moves in front of me.
- At L2, the opening's name and its checkpoint on a board with no names on its edge, until I hide it. No move list, and no departure or destination marked. Then a board in the starting position replaces it.
- At L3, the name only, in the question: "Rebuild the Italian Game." No move list, and no last move written out, since "3. Bc4" would give away a square. A board in the starting position, and no preview and no help.

**The question.** At L2 and L3: "Rebuild the Italian Game."

> **Stand-in.** Whether the name is also read aloud. I answer it later, with session 10. Every level plays in silence.

**How I answer.**

- At L1, I play each move on the board: the piece, then its square. The move is my answer as soon as I pick its square. A wrong move is corrected with the right piece's departure and destination, and I correct it and try again. Each correction is counted by its kind, and none fails the round.
- At L2 and L3, I move each piece the opening moved to its square, and take off each piece it took, then confirm. A piece moved wrong can go back before I confirm, and Reset puts the board back.

**What counts as right.** At L1, the line played whole from the starting position: the level ends when that reconstruction is right, and it never ends on a miss. At L2 and L3, the checkpoint, on all 64 squares, judged once I have rebuilt it whole and confirmed. The order I move pieces in never counts, nor which of two alike pieces I move: only where the pieces end. No part credit: one piece wrong in a known opening is a game begun on a wrong board.

**Where its questions come from.** The nine lines below. Each checkpoint is the position its line reaches, and no board is written by hand. A line that takes a piece shows the capture: the piece taken leaves the board.

**Levels.**

| Level | What I see first | How I answer | Questions per round | Minutes per round |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| L1 | The name, then each move on the board | Play each move, then rebuild it | 1 | None |
| L2 | The name, and the position on a board, until I hide it | Move the pieces | 1 | None |
| L3 | The name only | Move the pieces | 1 | None |

A question is one checkpoint, so a round is one question at every level. L1 teaches it, L2 asks it after a look, L3 asks it from its name alone. L1 goes at my own pace. L2 and L3 put accuracy before speed: no deadline for the round, and no countdown for the question.

**Scoring.** One round counts that I finished it, or got its question right or wrong. At L1 it also counts my time, and my corrections by kind; it has no pass mark. At L2 and L3 it counts right or wrong, my look time at L2 and my time to answer apart, and my mistakes by kind. A result is kept under the opening's challenge and its level, as one question.

> **Stand-in.** Pass mark for each opening's L2 and L3: the right answer in one round, and my look time at L2. My practice sets it. L1 has none.

**After each answer.** At L2 and L3, once the checkpoint is rebuilt whole and confirmed. The checkpoint shows, whole.

- Right: "Right: the Italian Game."
- Wrong, another opening's square: "No: in the Italian Game, White's bishop stands on c4. On b5, it is the Ruy Lopez."
- Wrong, a piece left at home: "No: in the Slav Defense, ...c6 was left out. Black's pawn is still on c7."
- Wrong, a piece taken left on: "No: in the Sicilian Defense, d2 is empty. White's pawn from d2 is gone, traded for Black's pawn from c7."

At L1, a wrong move is told in the right departure and destination alone: "Right piece. It goes to e4, not e3." When the reconstruction is right: "Right: the Italian Game, 1. e4 e5 2. Nf3 Nc6 3. Bc4. Anchor: White's bishop on c4."

**Mistakes it names.** One answer can show several. A wrong square takes the first kind it fits, in this order. A piece I never moved is told by the move I left out, before any square beside.

- A piece left at home: Black's pawn left on c7, in the Slav Defense, told by the move left out, "...c6". A forgotten piece, one of my handoff's five kinds (`02-learning-principles.md`).
- Chapter 1's five, on a piece put on a wrong square, against its right one. In the Italian Game, White's bishop belongs on c4: on d4, the file beside. On c3, the rank beside. On f5, the board turned. On f4, the files reversed. White's knight belongs on f3: on f6, the ranks reversed.
- Another opening's square: White's bishop on b5, for the Italian Game. That is the Ruy Lopez. It fits a piece that moves in this opening, put on a square another of the nine moved a piece to. A piece at home on its own square is no sign of any opening.
- A piece taken, left on: White's pawn left on d2, or Black's pawn left on c7 or c5, in the Sicilian Defense.
- A piece moved that stays home: Black's knight put on f6, in the Italian Game. It stays on g8.
- The wrong piece: White's knight on c4, in the Italian Game, where the bishop stands. A wrong piece, one of my handoff's five.
- Another square: any other wrong one.

At L1, a correction counts as the wrong piece, or another square.

**What comes back.** At L2 and L3, a miss comes back once in each of my next two rounds, in the same opening and at the same level: an Italian Game miss never becomes a Ruy Lopez question, and an L2 miss waits through the rounds of L1 and L3. Here the position itself is what I learn, so it comes back as itself. L1 feeds none.

**Examples.**

- **L1.** The starting position and "Italian Game". 1. e4: White's pawn goes from e2 to e4, e2 and e4 marked. "White occupies the centre with e4." I play it, then the next move, and so on to 3. Bc4. Then the whole line again from the starting position. Wrong: I move White's pawn to e3, and I am told "Right piece. It goes to e4, not e3." I try again.
- **L2.** "Rebuild the Italian Game." Its position shows, and I hide it. The board starts in the starting position. Right: I move White's pawn to e4, knight to f3 and bishop to c4, and Black's pawn to e5 and knight to c6. Wrong: I move the bishop to b5, the rest right, and I am told "No: in the Italian Game, White's bishop stands on c4. On b5, it is the Ruy Lopez."
- **L2.** "Rebuild the Sicilian Defense." Its position shows, and I hide it. Right: I take off White's pawn from d2 and Black's pawn from c7, move White's pawn to e4 and knight to d4, and Black's pawn to d6. Wrong: I leave White's pawn on d2, the rest right, and I am told "No: in the Sicilian Defense, d2 is empty. White's pawn from d2 is gone, traded for Black's pawn from c7."
- **L3.** "Rebuild the Slav Defense." Only the name shows. Right: I move White's pawns to d4 and c4, and Black's pawns to d5 and c6. Wrong: I leave Black's pawn on c7, the rest right, and I am told "No: in the Slav Defense, ...c6 was left out. Black's pawn is still on c7."

### 4.3.1 — Italian Game

**The line.** 1. e4 e5 2. Nf3 Nc6 3. Bc4

**What it teaches.**

- 1. e4: White occupies the centre with e4.
- 2. Nf3 and 2... Nc6: Both sides develop knights.
- 3. Bc4: White's bishop reaches c4 and attacks f7.

**Anchor.** White's bishop on c4.

**The checkpoint.** White: pawn e4, from e2; knight f3, from g1; bishop c4, from f1. Black: pawn e5, from e7; knight c6, from b8. 30 pieces besides the kings.

### 4.3.2 — Ruy Lopez

**The line.** 1. e4 e5 2. Nf3 Nc6 3. Bb5

**What it teaches.**

- 1. e4: The first four half-moves match the Italian Game.
- 3. Bb5: White's bishop reaches b5 instead of c4. It pressures Black's knight defending e5.

**Anchor.** White's bishop on b5.

**The checkpoint.** White: pawn e4, from e2; knight f3, from g1; bishop b5, from f1. Black: pawn e5, from e7; knight c6, from b8. 30 pieces besides the kings.

### 4.3.3 — Scotch Game

**The line.** 1. e4 e5 2. Nf3 Nc6 3. d4

**What it teaches.**

- 2. Nf3 and 2... Nc6: Both knights develop as in the Italian.
- 3. d4: White advances the d-pawn instead of developing the bishop. The pawn on d4 challenges Black's e5 pawn.

**Anchor.** White's pawn on d4.

**The checkpoint.** White: pawn e4, from e2; knight f3, from g1; pawn d4, from d2. Black: pawn e5, from e7; knight c6, from b8. 30 pieces besides the kings.

### 4.3.4 — Queen's Gambit

**The line.** 1. d4 d5 2. c4

**What it teaches.**

- 1. d4 and 1... d5: Both sides occupy the centre with d-pawns.
- 2. c4: White advances the c-pawn to c4. That pawn challenges Black's pawn on d5.

**Anchor.** White's pawns on d4 and c4.

**The checkpoint.** White: pawn d4, from d2; pawn c4, from c2. Black: pawn d5, from d7. 30 pieces besides the kings.

### 4.3.5 — London System

**The line.** 1. d4 d5 2. Nf3 Nf6 3. Bf4

**What it teaches.**

- 1. d4 and 1... d5: Both sides advance their d-pawns.
- 2. Nf3 and 2... Nf6: Both kingside knights develop.
- 3. Bf4: White develops its c1 bishop to f4.

**Anchor.** White's bishop on f4.

**The checkpoint.** White: pawn d4, from d2; knight f3, from g1; bishop f4, from c1. Black: pawn d5, from d7; knight f6, from g8. 30 pieces besides the kings.

### 4.3.6 — Sicilian Defense

**The line.** 1. e4 c5 2. Nf3 d6 3. d4 cxd4 4. Nxd4

**What it teaches.**

- 1... c5: Black answers asymmetrically with ...c5.
- 3. d4: White challenges the centre with d4.
- 3... cxd4: Black captures White's d-pawn. The white d-pawn disappears.
- 4. Nxd4: White's f3 knight recaptures on d4. The black c-pawn also disappears.

**Anchor.** White's knight on d4.

**The checkpoint.** White: pawn e4, from e2; knight d4, from g1; the pawn from d2 gone. Black: pawn d6, from d7; the pawn from c7 gone. 28 pieces besides the kings.

### 4.3.7 — French Defense

**The line.** 1. e4 e6 2. d4 d5

**What it teaches.**

- 1. e4 and 2. d4: White builds a centre with e4 and d4.
- 1... e6: Black prepares ...d5 with ...e6.
- 2... d5: The central pawn structure defines this checkpoint.

**Anchor.** Black's pawn on e6.

**The checkpoint.** White: pawn e4, from e2; pawn d4, from d2. Black: pawn e6, from e7; pawn d5, from d7. 30 pieces besides the kings.

### 4.3.8 — Caro-Kann Defense

**The line.** 1. e4 c6 2. d4 d5

**What it teaches.**

- 1. e4 and 2. d4: White establishes e4 and d4.
- 1... c6: Black prepares ...d5 using ...c6.
- 2... d5: The c6 pawn supports Black's d5 pawn.

**Anchor.** Black's pawns on c6 and d5.

**The checkpoint.** White: pawn e4, from e2; pawn d4, from d2. Black: pawn c6, from c7; pawn d5, from d7. 30 pieces besides the kings.

### 4.3.9 — Slav Defense

**The line.** 1. d4 d5 2. c4 c6

**What it teaches.**

- 2. c4: White plays the Queen's Gambit structure.
- 2... c6: Black answers with ...c6. Black's c6 pawn reinforces the d5 pawn.

**Anchor.** Black's pawns on c6 and d5.

**The checkpoint.** White: pawn d4, from d2; pawn c4, from c2. Black: pawn d5, from d7; pawn c6, from c7. 30 pieces besides the kings.

Each line stands on the move or moves it speaks of; a line that names the first four half-moves stands on the first. Every other move shows only its departure and destination.
