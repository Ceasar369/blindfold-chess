# Chapter 2 — Piece movement

Each piece's moves, held in my head: where a knight lands, where a line stops, what a piece attacks, and where it may go.

A level is one step of a challenge's difficulty: it sets each dial, such as how many pieces stand on the board or the time per question, to an exact value.

A round is one sitting at one level: a fixed number of questions, ending on my score, or sooner when its minutes run out.

## What I can do at the end

- I name every square a knight reaches, from any square, with no board, within 15 seconds. Every knight move in a blindfold game asks it, mine and my opponent's.
- I find a shortest knight route between two squares, with no board, around my own pawns. Every knight manoeuvre I plan blind asks it.
- I name the enemy pieces a rook, bishop or queen attacks, from a position given in words. After each move my opponent makes, I ask what that piece now hits.
- I name every piece of one side that attacks a square, or gives check, from a position given in words. Before I put a piece on a square, I ask it.
- I name every square a pawn or a king may move to, check included, from a position given in words. Every pawn push and every king step asks it, escaping check most of all.

Unproven: that these drills make my blindfold play better. No study found tests piece-movement drills (my references, `drafts-and-science/what-it-means.md`).

## What it needs first

These levels of chapter 1:

- 1.1.1 L2 — finding a square on a board with no names. Every click here asks it.
- 1.2.1 L1 — a square's colour. A knight always lands on the other colour, and 2.1's feedback says so.
- 1.3.1 L1 — the squares along a diagonal. A bishop's and a queen's lines build on it.
- 1.3.1 L3 — the same, with no board, before any line is followed blind.

Each card names the levels it needs. No level needs chapter 1 finished.

## The sections

**2.1 — Knight jumps.** The squares a knight lands on, and its routes. 2.1.1 Name the knight's squares. 2.1.2 Find the knight's route.

**2.2 — Lines.** Where a rook's, bishop's or queen's line stops, and what it hits. 2.2.1 Name what it attacks.

**2.3 — Pieces together.** Every piece of one side at once: who attacks a square, and where a pawn or a king may go. 2.3.1 Name who attacks a square. 2.3.2 Name where it may move.

**Reach, attack, may move to.** Three words, each defined once here. Every question says which it asks.

- **Reach.** The squares a piece moves to on an empty board. A knight on e4 reaches c3, c5, d2, d6, f2, f6, g3 and g5. A white pawn on e2 reaches e3 and e4.
- **Attack.** A piece attacks a square where it could capture an enemy piece (FIDE Laws, 3.1.2). A line stops at the first piece on it, of either side: an enemy piece there is attacked, a piece of its own side is not. A pawn attacks the two squares diagonally ahead of it, never the one in front. A piece still attacks a square when moving there would expose its own king (FIDE Laws, 3.1.3).
- **May move to.** Where the rules let it go now. Never onto a piece of its own side, never over any piece. A pawn moves one square straight ahead onto an empty square, or two from its starting rank, White's second or Black's seventh, when both are empty. It moves one square diagonally ahead only to capture. A king never moves where an enemy piece would attack it.
- **One real case.** The kings on g1 and g8. White's pawn on e4 faces Black's knight on d5 and Black's pawn on e5: it reaches e5, attacks d5 and f5, and may move to d5 only. Take those away, and put Black's rook on a2: White's king reaches f1, f2, g2, h1 and h2, and may move to f1 and h1.

**The side I sit on.** Where a board shows, I sit as White, in every challenge, and the board faces White. Black's pieces are asked about in 2.2 and 2.3 as often as White's, so Black's pawns go down the board here too. Every last level has no board until I reveal an empty one, and no seat.

**Rules beyond one piece's move** (FIDE Laws, 3.7 to 3.9):

- Check — trained. A king attacked is in check (FIDE Laws, 3.9.1). 2.3.1 asks who attacks a king's square, and 2.3.2 where a checked king may go.
- Castling — not on this page. Its right depends on whether the king or the rook has moved (FIDE Laws, 3.8.2.1), which a position never shows. A king on e1 beside its rook lists only the squares next to it.
- A pawn taken in passing — not on this page. In passing, "en passant": a pawn that has just moved two squares is taken by an enemy pawn beside it, as if it had moved one. It is allowed only on the very next move (FIDE Laws, 3.7.3.2), and a position never shows the last move.
- Promotion — not on this page. No pawn asked about in 2.3.2 stands one step from its last rank. A pawn's attack on a last-rank square still counts in 2.3.1.

**From my outline's six draft sections** (my handoff, `03-curriculum-outline.md`):

- Rook movement — merged into 2.2. A rook's file and rank are chapter 1's map. What is new is where its line stops.
- Bishop movement — merged into 2.2. A bishop's open diagonal is 1.3.1's. 2.2.1 adds the pieces that stop it. One learner found the queen and the bishop the hardest pieces to track (`drafts-and-science/drafts/blog-marcus-folkesson-20-days-to-a-blindfold-game.md`).
- Queen and king movement — split. The queen joins 2.2: she moves as a rook and a bishop together (`existing-courses-and-drills/chess-com-spigowis-how-to-move-the-pieces.md`). The king joins 2.3: his steps are the squares next to him, and earn a challenge only where enemy pieces forbid some.
- Knight movement — kept, as 2.1, in two challenges: its squares, and its routes. One coach found knight moves and diagonals harder than remembering moves (`drafts-and-science/drafts/chessfox-blindfold-course.md`).
- Pawn movement and attacks — merged into 2.3: its attacks in 2.3.1, its moves in 2.3.2. Both depend on the pieces around it.
- Blockers, attacks, and interacting pieces — kept, as 2.3. Blockers come sooner, from 2.2.1's first level: an open line is 1.3.1's already. No draft found has a blocker drill (`drafts-and-science/what-it-means.md`).

## The challenges

### 2.1.1 — Name the knight's squares

**It trains.** Seeing every square a knight reaches, at once.

**It needs.** 1.1.1 L2 and 1.2.1 L1.

**What I see.** At L1 and L2, an empty board with no names on its edge, and one knight on its square. At L3, the same board with nothing on it. At L4 and L5, no board until I reveal an empty one. Where a board shows, I sit as White.

**The question.** "Which squares can a knight on e4 reach?"

> **Stand-in.** Whether the question is also read aloud. I answer it later, with session 10. Every level plays in silence.

**How I answer.** At L1 to L3, I click each square, then confirm. At L4 and L5, I press "Reveal board", click each square on the empty board, then confirm. A square clicked again comes off.

**What counts as right.** Exactly the knight's squares: none missing, none extra. Their order never counts. No part credit: a knight square missed in a game is a fork I never saw.

**Where its questions come from.** One knight, alone. At L1, it stands on one of the 16 centre squares, c3 to f6, where it always reaches 8. From L2, on any of the 64, at random: near an edge or a corner, its squares drop to 6, 4, 3 or 2. Never on the file or the rank of the square before. Knights coming back take places among them.

**Levels.**

| Level | What the board shows | Where the knight stands | How I answer | Time per question | Questions per round | Minutes per round |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| L1 | The knight | The centre 16 | Click | None | 10 | 3 |
| L2 | The knight | Any of the 64 | Click | None | 10 | 3 |
| L3 | Nothing | Any of the 64 | Click | None | 10 | 3 |
| L4 | No board until I reveal it | Any of the 64 | Click | None | 10 | 4 |
| L5 | No board until I reveal it | Any of the 64 | Click | 15 seconds | 10 | 3 |

Ten questions, since each asks for up to eight squares. Fifteen seconds asks for them at a glance. Another trainer asks the same: click every square a knight can reach (`github-trainers/pages/caltho--blindfold-chess-trainer.md`).

> **Stand-in.** L5's 15 seconds hold for now, provisional. Eight squares take 10 clicks, Reveal board and Confirm included. Unproven: that the clicks fit in 15 seconds. Nothing has timed it.

**Scoring.** One round counts my right answers out of 10, my average time per answer, and my mistakes by kind. At L5, no answer within 15 seconds is a miss: too slow. A question left when the minutes run out is a miss.

> **Stand-in.** Pass mark for 2.1.1 L1 to L5: the right answers in one round, and my average time per answer. My practice sets it.

**After each answer.**

- Right: "Right: c3, c5, d2, d6, f2, f6, g3 and g5."
- Wrong, I gave g4 and left out g3: "No: a knight on e4 reaches c3, c5, d2, d6, f2, f6, g3 and g5. g4 is the rank beside g3."
- Wrong, I gave e6 too: "No: a knight on e4 reaches c3, c5, d2, d6, f2, f6, g3 and g5. e6 is light, like e4: a knight always lands on the other colour."

**Mistakes it names.** Each is a wrong square, one of my handoff's five kinds (`02-learning-principles.md`). One answer can show several. A wrong square takes the first kind it fits.

- A square left out: g3, for a knight on e4.
- Chapter 1's five, against a square I left out. With g3 left out, for a knight on e4: f3, the file beside. g4, the rank beside. b6, the board turned: b6 sits in g3's place when I sit at the other side. b3, the files reversed: I counted the files from the wrong edge. g6, the ranks reversed: I counted the ranks from the wrong edge.
- Too slow: no answer within 15 seconds, at L5.
- A square of the knight's own colour: e6, for a knight on e4. Both are light. One course gives this check: each knight move must change colour (`drafts-and-science/drafts/saychess-invisible-chess-10-week-course.md`).
- Another square: any other wrong one, such as e5 for a knight on e4.

**What comes back.** A question I miss comes back once in each of my next two rounds holding this challenge, the knight on the same square.

- **L4.** No board. "Which squares can a knight on g2 reach?" Right: I press Reveal board, click e1, e3, f4 and h4, then confirm. Wrong: I also click g3 before I confirm, and I am told "No: a knight on g2 reaches e1, e3, f4 and h4. g3 is no knight's square: it sits next to g2."

### 2.1.2 — Find the knight's route

**It trains.** Steering a knight between two squares in the fewest moves.

**It needs.** 2.1.1 L3. From L5, 2.1.1 L4.

**What I see.** At L1 and L2, an empty board with no names on its edge, my knight on its start square, and the target lit. At L3, the same board, with my two pawns on it too, all question long. At L4, the same board with nothing on it: never the knight, the target or the pawns. At L5, no board until I reveal an empty one. Where a board shows, I sit as White.

**The question.** "Take your knight from b1 to d4 in the fewest moves." Where my pawns are in the question, it names them: "Take your knight from b1 to e5 in the fewest moves. Your pawns stand on a3 and f3."

> **Stand-in.** Whether the question is also read aloud. I answer it later, with session 10. Every level plays in silence.

**How I answer.** At L1 to L4, I click each square my knight lands on, in order, ending on the target, then confirm. At L5, I press "Reveal board" first, then click them the same way. The start square may lead, or not.

**What counts as right.** A route where each step is a knight move, ending on the target, in the fewest moves. Any route doing that counts. It never lands on a pawn: my own pieces block a landing, never a jump. Here the order counts: a route is a sequence, not a set. No part credit.

**Where its questions come from.** Two squares at random, as many knight moves apart on an empty board as the level sets. Two squares of different colours lie an odd number of moves apart, two of the same colour an even number (`existing-courses-and-drills/chessprogramming-knight-distance.md`). Where my pawns are in the question, they are two, and neither is decorative: each stands on a shortest route the other pawn leaves open, so each blocks one the other does not. One shortest route always stays free, so the pawns never make the way longer. Two squares 2 moves apart have 2 shortest routes at most, so a pawn question takes 3 or 4 moves, and at L4, 3. They never stand on the start, the target, rank 1 or rank 8. At L3, every question has pawns. At L4 and L5, 4 of a round's 8 do, placed at random in the round. Starts and targets vary. Pairs coming back take places among them, and come back with their own pawns.

**Levels.**

| Level | What the board shows | Fewest moves | My pawns in the way | How I answer | Questions per round | Minutes per round |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| L1 | My knight and the target | 2 | None | Click | 8 | 2 |
| L2 | My knight and the target | 3 | None | Click | 8 | 3 |
| L3 | My knight, the target and my two pawns, all question long | 3 | 2, in every question | Click | 8 | 4 |
| L4 | An empty board, no edge names: never the knight, the target or the pawns | 2 or 3 | 2, in 4 of 8 questions | Click | 8 | 5 |
| L5 | No board until I reveal it | 3 or 4 | 2, in 4 of 8 questions | Click | 8 | 5 |

No clock: a route is planned, not glanced at. Routes stop at 4 moves: past that, a question is mostly clicking. Eight questions, since a route asks for up to four squares in order. Coaches and other trainers give the same drill, a route between two squares in the fewest moves (`existing-courses-and-drills/chess-com-stamma1-how-to-play-blindfold-chess.md`, `existing-courses-and-drills/chessfactor-kavutskiy-top-3-visualization-methods.md`, `existing-courses-and-drills/chess-com-jugadabrillante-learn-to-play-blindfold-chess.md`, `github-trainers/pages/ariera--chess-viz-trainer.md`, `github-trainers/pages/checkmate-works--blindfold-chess.md`). A race past pawns is one blog's harder version (`existing-courses-and-drills/chess-com-stamma1-how-to-play-blindfold-chess.md`).

**Scoring.** One round counts my right answers out of 8, my average time per answer, and my mistakes by kind. A question left when the minutes run out is a miss.

> **Stand-in.** Pass mark for 2.1.2 L1 to L5: the right answers in one round. My practice sets it.

**After each answer.**

- Right: "Right: d2, f3, d4, in 3 moves."
- Wrong, a jump that is no knight move: "No: c3 to e3 is no knight move. e2, the rank beside, was. One route: b1, c3, e2, d4."
- Wrong, a longer route: "No: b1 to d4 takes 3 moves, and yours took 5. One route: b1, d2, f3, d4. b1 is light and d4 dark, so the count is odd."

**Mistakes it names.** A wrong route takes the first kind it fits.

- A jump that is no knight move: c3 to e3, on the way from b1 to d4. A move read wrongly, one of my handoff's five kinds (`02-learning-principles.md`).
- Chapter 1's five name that wrong landing against a square that works from the same place. From c3 toward d4, e2 and b5 work: f2, the file beside e2. e3, the rank beside e2. d7, e2 with the board turned. g5, b5 with the files reversed. e7, e2 with the ranks reversed.
- A landing on my pawn: a3, holding my pawn, on the way from b1 to e5. A forgotten piece, one of my handoff's five.
- A route ending elsewhere: b1, c3, e4, f6, when the target is g5. A sequence lost, one of my handoff's five.
- A longer route: 5 moves from b1 to d4, where 3 do it.

**What comes back.** A pair I miss comes back once in each of my next two rounds holding this challenge.

**Examples.**

Wrong: I click d2, f3, e5, c6 and d4, and I am told "No: b1 to d4 takes 3 moves, and yours took 5. One route: b1, d2, f3, d4. b1 is light and d4 dark, so the count is odd."

The only route: b1, d2, c4, e5."
- **L4.** Nothing on the board. "Take your knight from c1 to g6 in the fewest moves." Right: I click d3, f4 and g6. Wrong: I click d3, f5 and g6, and I am told "No: d3 to f5 is no knight move. e5, the file beside, was. One route: c1, d3, e5, g6."

**It needs.** 1.3.1 L1. From L4, 1.3.1 L3.

At L3 and L4, the position is in words, which stays on screen all question long. White's pieces come first, then Black's, each side's king first: "White: king g1, queen e2, pawns e4 and f2. Black: king g8, rook e8, bishop b5, knight h5."

**The question.** "Which of Black's pieces does White's bishop on c4 attack?"

> **Stand-in.** Whether the question is also read aloud. I answer it later, with session 10. Every level plays in silence.

**How I answer.** At L1 and L2, I click each piece it attacks, then confirm, or I pick None. The pieces on the board are the choices. An enemy piece there is attacked. A piece of its own side is not, and blocks it. The enemy king counts: attacking it is check. No part credit: one piece missed is a piece left hanging, or a check missed.

**Where its questions come from.** Positions set up for the drill, never from real play: a real position holds up to 32 pieces, and this challenge trains lines, not holding a position. Each holds both kings and the level's count of other pieces, the piece asked about among them, of both sides, at random. The piece asked about is White's or Black's, at random. At least two other pieces stand on its lines. At least one stands behind another piece on one of its lines. Each new question attacks nothing one time in five, decided on its own: a round may hold none, or several. A question coming back keeps its own answer. The kings never stand side by side, and never both in check. No pawn stands on the first or the eighth rank.

### 2.3.1 — Name who attacks a square

**It trains.** Finding every piece of one side that attacks one square.

**It needs.** 2.1.1 L2 and 2.2.1 L2. From L5, 2.2.1 L4.

**What I see.** At L1 to L3, a board with no names on its edge, both kings and the other pieces on it, the square asked about lit. I sit as White. At L4, an empty board with no names on its edge: only the square asked about is lit, and no piece shows. At L5, no board until I reveal an empty one, nothing lit. At L4 and L5, the position is in words, which stays on screen all question long, White's pieces first, each side's king first.

**The question.** "Which of White's pieces attack e5?"

> **Stand-in.** Whether the question is also read aloud. I answer it later, with session 10. Every level plays in silence.

**How I answer.** At L1 to L3, I click each piece that attacks it, then confirm, or I pick None. The pieces on the board are the choices. At L4, I click their squares on the empty board, then confirm, or I pick None. At L5, I press "Reveal board", click their squares, then confirm, or I pick None, which needs no reveal.

**What counts as right.** Exactly the pieces of the side named that attack the square: none missing, none extra. Their order never counts. A knight a jump away attacks it. A pawn diagonally behind it does: White's pawns on d4 and f4 attack e5, Black's on d6 and f6. A king beside it does. A rook, bishop or queen does from its line, with nothing between. A pawn straight behind it never does. When the square holds the other side's king, each piece attacking it gives check. No part credit: one attacker missed is a piece I put where it can be taken.

**Where its questions come from.** Positions set up for the drill, never from real play, for 2.2.1's reason. Each holds both kings and the level's count of other pieces, of both sides, at random. The side asked about is White's or Black's, at random. The square is empty or holds a piece of the other side, never one of the side asked about. 0 to 3 pieces attack it. Each new question has no attacker one time in five, decided on its own: a round may hold none, or several. A question coming back keeps its own answer. At least one piece of the side asked about looks like an attacker and is not: blocked on its line, or a pawn straight behind the square. ### 2.3.2 — Name where it may move

**It trains.** Knowing every square a pawn or a king may move to, among other pieces.

**It needs.** 1.1.1 L2. From L2, 2.3.1 L1. From L3, 2.3.1 L3. From L5, 2.3.1 L5.

**What I see.** At L1 to L3, a board with no names on its edge, both kings and the other pieces on it, the piece asked about lit. I sit as White. At L4, an empty board with no names on its edge: only the square of the piece asked about is lit, and no piece shows. At L5, no board until I reveal an empty one, nothing lit. At L4 and L5, the position is in words, which stays on screen all question long, White's pieces first, each side's king first. At L3 and L4, a king asked about in check is said to be, and from which pieces. At L5, nothing says so.

**The question.** "Which squares may White's pawn on d2 move to?" Or, of a king: "Which squares may Black's king on e8 move to?"

> **Stand-in.** Whether the question is also read aloud. I answer it later, with session 10. Every level plays in silence.

**How I answer.** At L1 to L3, I click each square, then confirm, or I pick None. At L4, I click each square on the empty board, then confirm, or I pick None. At L5, I press "Reveal board", click each square, then confirm, or I pick None, which needs no reveal.

**What counts as right.** Exactly the squares it may move to: none missing, none extra. Their order never counts.

- A pawn: one square straight ahead, if empty. Two from its starting rank, White's second or Black's seventh, if both are empty. One square diagonally ahead, only onto an enemy piece. Never off a line it shields its own king along: it may still move along that line, or take the piece it shields the king from.
- A king: each square next to it, empty or holding an enemy piece, where no enemy piece would attack it once it stands there. That rules out an enemy piece another guards, and the square behind the king on an enemy's line: once the king steps away, it no longer blocks that line. Castling never counts.

"None" is right when it may move nowhere. No part credit: one wrong square is an illegal move.

**Where its questions come from.** Positions set up for the drill, never from real play, for 2.2.1's reason. Each holds both kings and 4 other pieces, of both sides, at random. The side asked about is White's or Black's, at random.

- A pawn asked about stands on White's ranks 2 to 6, or Black's 7 to 3. In three of every ten pawn questions, it stands on its starting rank. Pieces of both sides stand ahead of it and diagonally ahead, at random. Its own king is not in check. At L1, no move of the pawn would leave it in check. From L3, each new pawn question holds a pawn shielding its king from a rook, bishop or queen one time in three, decided on its own: along a file, a rank or a diagonal. The laws of chess decide which of its moves stay legal.
- A king asked about has enemy pieces attacking some of its squares, and pieces of its own side on some. From L3, it stands in check in 3 of the 5 king questions. Only the king asked about may stand in check.

The kings never stand side by side. No pawn stands on the first or the eighth rank.

- 2.2.1 L4.

- 2.3.2 L5.
