# Chapter 5 — Updating a position

A position held in my head while moves change it: each move shown once, and nothing on screen renewing the board.

A level is one step of a challenge's difficulty: it sets each dial, such as how many moves come or how often they stop for a question, to an exact value.

A round is one sitting at one level: a fixed number of questions, ending on my score, or sooner when its minutes run out.

## What I can do at the end

- I follow a real game from the starting position through move 10, each move shown once, and name what stands on any square its moves changed, with no board. Before each move in a blindfold game, I ask what stands where my piece would land.
- I rebuild the whole board after a real game's first 10 moves, each side's move shown once. In a blindfold game, stopping to set every piece in place is how I check the board in my head, as the Stepping Stones method does (my references, `drafts-and-science/drafts/saychess-invisible-chess-viii-stepping-stones.md`).
- I keep an exchange straight through a game's first 10 moves: which piece took, which took back, and which are gone. By move 10, 8,540 of 10,400 real games at my level hold one (`updating-a-position-lookups/lichess-games-2013-01-sequences.md`).

Unproven: that these drills make my blindfold play better. Players say they lose the thread of a blindfold game after about 10 moves (`drafts-and-science/drafts/blog-marcus-folkesson-20-days-to-a-blindfold-game.md`, his first game), 10 to 12 (`drafts-and-science/drafts/darksquares-30-day-beginner-plan.md`, unsourced), about 14 (`drafts-and-science/drafts/chesscom-forum-how-many-moves-blindfolded-gigante-2026.md`), or 20 to 30 (`drafts-and-science/drafts/lichess-forum-any-tips-on-blindfold-chess.md`). This chapter ends at move 10, the earliest of them. Self-reports, all of them.

## The sections

**5.1 — One square, after the moves.** A position held while moves change it, asked one square at a time. 5.1.1 Name what stands there now.

**5.2 — The whole board, after the moves.** The same, asked whole. 5.2.1 Rebuild after the moves.

**Two answers.** Naming one square and rebuilding the whole board give different answers, so they are two challenges. A game asks one square at a time, before each move I make: what stands where my piece would land. It asks the whole board when I stop to check it. Where I lost the thread is a third answer, but no challenge asks it of me: the page finds it from my answer, after a miss, as below. A game never asks me to name it, and the page can find it exactly.

**Half-moves.** A half-move is one side's move: 1. e4 e5 holds two. 20 half-moves run through move 10.

**How the moves reach me.** One at a time. Each move shows alone, numbered as the game numbers it, "2. Nf3" or "2... d6", until I ask for the next. Then it is gone, and nothing shows the position. A blindfold game hands me each move once. Moves read as chapter 3 shows them, x, + and # included.

The whole list at once comes in one level only, 5.2.1 L1: "1. e4 e5 2. Nf3 Nf6 3. Nxe5 Nc6", until I hide it. On my first day, I may read a move twice. One course reads four moves a side, then shows the position (`existing-courses-and-drills/chessfactor-astaneh-visualization-series-part-2.md`). One player's advice starts with the move list in sight, then takes it away (`drafts-and-science/drafts/chesscom-blog-cuzinvinny-blindfold-chess-journal.md`).

**Where the first position comes from.** Always one I hold by heart, so a miss points at the moves, never at a look.

- The starting position, at every level but one. Every blindfold game starts there, and 4.1.1 L1 trained it.
- An opening by its name, at 5.2.1 L3: one of 4.3.1's openings, then the game's moves go on from it. Chapter 4 holds an opening as the starting position and a few changes. Here the game goes past it.
- A position seen once: never. A blindfold game never shows me one. Holding one is chapter 4's.

**Where the moves come from.** Every sequence is real: the first moves of a game people played. Lichess's game database, games between players rated 1000 to 1500, of 5 minutes or more each, that ended normally (`content-and-progress/content/lichess-standard-games-database.md`), as chapter 4 takes its positions. A sequence is a game's first 6, 8, 12 or 20 half-moves, as the level sets, from a game that goes on past it. At 5.2.1 L3, it is the 6 half-moves right after the game reaches the opening. No trainer found grades real sequences by length (`reuse-digest/by-chapter.md`): these lengths are mine. **Captures, exchanges and special moves.** They come as the games played them, from each challenge's first level. An exchange is a capture taken straight back on the same square: 4. dxe5 dxe5. By the 20th half-move, of 10,400 such games, 9,728 hold a capture, 8,540 an exchange, and 4,890 a castling (`updating-a-position-lookups/lichess-games-2013-01-sequences.md`). They are common at my level, so no level adds them: they come as often as a game holds them. One guide finds exchanges break the picture in the head, and drills a capture and its recapture first (`drafts-and-science/drafts/chessworld-net-blindfold-safe-progression.md`). Here 5.1.1 can ask any square an exchange changed.

A pawn taken in passing, in French "en passant", is a pawn that has just moved two squares, taken by an enemy pawn beside it as if it had moved one. It is rare, and so is a promotion: 63 and 8 of those 10,400 games by the 20th half-move. They come only as games play them, never picked: a blindfold game meets them as rarely. Chapter 3 trained their squares, 3.1.1 L3 to L6. Lichess's puzzle file holds fewer still at my level: in one sample, no puzzle rated under 1000 holds a castling or a pawn taken in passing (`content-and-progress/content/lichess-puzzle-themes-and-counts.md`). It is no source here.

**Checkpoints.** A checkpoint is where the moves stop, and 5.1.1 asks before they go on. They thin out across its levels.

- After every 2 half-moves, each full move, at L1 and L2. One draft plays one move in the head, checks, then adds a second (`drafts-and-science/drafts/chessdna-visualization-skill-ladder.md`).
- After every 6, three moves a side, at L3. The Stepping Stones method stops every one to three moves, and checks every piece before going on (`drafts-and-science/drafts/saychess-invisible-chess-viii-stepping-stones.md`, `existing-courses-and-drills/saychess-invisible-chess-newcomers-guide.md`).
- After every 10, five moves a side, at L4. One player's ladder starts at about 5 moves a side, then 7, then 10 (`existing-courses-and-drills/chess-com-d34l10-blindfold-tips.md`). A forum's drill plays 5 moves a side in the head, then checks on a second board (`existing-courses-and-drills/chess-com-forum-blindfold-and-coordinate-threads.md`).

5.2.1 never stops mid-way. Each sequence ends on its one rebuild, and the position shows after it. A stop mid-way would show the whole board, renewing it. Its stretches grow instead: 6, then 12, then 20 half-moves.

**The look, the wait, the clock.** Each move shows as long as I like: no level caps it. A game gives me my own time to take a move in, and holding, not speed, is trained here. The list at 5.2.1 L1 shows as long as I like too. My average time per move is counted, and the pass marks read it. No level adds a wait: the later moves are the wait, as I hold an early change while more come. No level times an answer. My average time per answer is counted. Chapter 4 does the same, for the same reason.

**The side I sit on.** Where a board shows, at 5.1.1 L1 and at every level of 5.2.1, I sit on a side picked at random each round, kept for the whole round, as chapter 3 does. A game seats me either side. The board faces White whichever side I play. The moves are both sides', as a game's record reads. A level with no board has no seat.

**Moves read aloud.** Whether the app reads each move aloud waits for me: each card carries a stand-in at its question. Every level plays in silence. Masters recalled games heard as well as games seen: strong players only, read second-hand (`drafts-and-science/science/campitelli-gobet-2005-minds-eye-blindfold.md`).

**Where I lost the thread.** A sequence lost is one of my handoff's five mistake kinds (`02-learning-principles.md`). My references found no source that records it (`reuse-digest/by-chapter.md`). Here the page finds the move where I lost it.

- One square, in 5.1.1. My answer is what stood on the square before a later move changed it, once or more. The move counted is the last one that took my answer off the square. That move is the one I missed. In 1. e4 e5 2. Nf3 d6 3. d4 Nc6 4. dxe5 dxe5, "White's pawn" for e5 names 4... dxe5.
- The whole board, in 5.2.1. Each square I get wrong held my answer before, once or more. For each, the move counted is the last one that took my answer off the square. That move is one I missed. The earliest of those moves is where I lost the thread. A square that never held my answer names no move: I swapped two pieces, or held the first position wrong.

The feedback names that move. The round counts the moves I lost, by their kind: a move, a capture, castling, or a pawn taken in passing.

**What this page leaves out.** Choosing my own moves, and finding what they win: every move here is given to me. Whose move it is, and whether castling is still allowed: chapter 3 leaves castling's rule out, and every castling here is legal as played. A position seen once, for the reason above.

## The challenges

### 5.1.1 — Name what stands there now

**It trains.** Finding what stands on one square, in a position held in my head while moves change it.

**It needs.** 3.1.1 L6, 4.1.1 L1 and 1.1.1 L2. From L2, 4.1.1 L4.

**What I see.** Nothing before the first move: the sequence starts from the starting position, which I hold. Then each move alone, numbered as the game numbers it, until I ask for the next. Then it is gone. Nothing shows the position. At each checkpoint, the moves stop and its questions show, one at a time. At L1, an empty board with no names on its edge, the square asked lit. The board faces White whichever side I play. From L2, no board: the square is only named.

**The question.** "What stands on e5 now?"

> **Stand-in.** Whether each move, and the question, is also read aloud. I answer it later, with session 10. Every level plays in silence.

**How I answer.** At every level, I pick one of 13, as in 4.1.1: White's king, queen, rook, bishop, knight or pawn, Black's the same, or Empty. Then the moves go on.

**What counts as right.** What stands on the square after the last move shown: its side and its kind, or Empty. No part credit: the right piece on the wrong side is a piece I would take for mine.

**Where its questions come from.** Real sequences, as the sections say: a game's first 8 half-moves at L1 and L2, 12 at L3, 20 at L4. No game twice in a round.

Each checkpoint asks one square for every two half-moves since the one before: 1 at L1 and L2, 3 at L3, 5 at L4. Half of them, rounded up, are squares the moves since the last checkpoint changed. The rest are squares earlier moves changed, and none since. At the first checkpoint, all are its own. A square changes when a piece leaves it, lands on it, or is taken from it, as chapter 3 says. No square is asked twice in one sequence. A square no move changed is never asked: chapter 4 already asks it.

Sequences coming back take places among them.

**Levels.**

| L1 | 8 | 2 half-moves | An empty board, the square lit | Piece picker | 12 | 4 |

**After each answer.** Each names the move that last changed the square, and a miss says why, by its kind below. Explanations helped more than "wrong" alone, in computer lessons at school and college (`drafts-and-science/science/van-der-kleij-2015-feedback-computer-meta-analysis.md`).

- Wrong, a capture missed: "No: Black's pawn stands on e5, since 4... dxe5. White's pawn stood there until 4... dxe5 took it."
- Wrong, a piece that moved again: "No: f3 is empty since 4. Nxd4. White's knight stood there until 4. Nxd4 moved it."
- Wrong, a piece one square off: "No: Black's knight stands on c6, since 3... Nc6. Black's pawn stands on c7, the rank beside."

**Mistakes it names.** A wrong answer takes the first kind it fits. An old square comes first: here, what stood on the square before a move explains a miss better than a piece one square off. After 4... dxe5 in L1's example, "White's pawn" for e5 fits both, since White's pawn stands on e4, the rank beside.

- An old square: my answer stood there before a later move changed it, once or more, and the page names the last move that took my answer off the square. White's pawn, for e5, in L1's example: 4... dxe5 took it. Black's pawn, for d6: 4... dxe5 moved it. A sequence lost, one of my handoff's five kinds (`02-learning-principles.md`).
- Chapter 1's five, by where the piece I named stands now. In L1's example, after 4... dxe5: for c6, Black's pawn, on c7, the rank beside. For f3, Black's knight, on c6, the board turned.
- A piece left out: Empty, for a square never empty through the sequence. Empty, for d8, in L3's example, where Black's queen, then White's queen, then Black's king stood. A forgotten piece, one of my handoff's five.
- The wrong side: White's queen, for f6, in L2's example, where Black's queen stands.
- The wrong piece: White's bishop, for f3, in L1's example, where White's knight stands. A wrong piece, one of my handoff's five.
- A piece where nothing stands: Black's knight, for d7, in L1's example. d7 is empty since 2... d6, and no Black knight stood there or stands one square off it.
- Another answer: any other wrong one, such as Black's bishop, for f3, in L1's example.

> **Stand-in.** Whether each move, and at L3 the opening's name, is also read aloud. I answer it later, with session 10. Every level plays in silence.

## The path

Each step is one level, in this order.

1. 5.2.1 L1 — Rebuild after the moves, the list read whole.
2. 5.1.1 L1 — Name what stands there now, a checkpoint every full move.
3. 5.2.1 L2 — Rebuild after the moves, one at a time.
4. 5.1.1 L2 — Name what stands there now, no board.
5. 5.2.1 L3 — Rebuild after the moves, from an opening by its name.
6. 5.1.1 L3 — Name what stands there now, 12 half-moves.
7. 5.2.1 L4 — Rebuild after the moves, 12 half-moves.
8. 5.1.1 L4 — Name what stands there now, through move 10.
9. 5.2.1 L5 — Rebuild after the moves, through move 10.

The order climbs in tiers: every challenge's first levels come before any challenge's last one. The list comes first: on my first day, I may read a move twice. At 12 and at 20 half-moves, one square at a time comes before the whole board, which asks every square at once.

No round mixes the two. Mixing helped when the things mixed look alike, and lost to practising one at a time for word lists (`drafts-and-science/science/brunmair-richter-2019-interleaving-meta-analysis.md`). These two ask different answers, not alike ones to tell apart. And each last level already asks as a game does: each move shown once, nothing on screen showing what I hold. Unproven whether a mixed round would help or hurt here.

Reaching step 9's pass mark ends the chapter.
