# Chapter 1 — Board coordinates

The board's map, held in my head: where each square sits, its name, its colour, its diagonals.

A level is one step of a challenge's difficulty: it sets each dial, such as the names on the edge or the time per question, to an exact value.

A round is one sitting at one level: a fixed number of questions, ending on my score, or sooner when its minutes run out.

## What I can do at the end

- I find any square named to me on a blank board within 2 seconds. Every move in a blindfold game names a square I must place.
- I name any square I picture within 5 seconds. Every move I make in a blindfold game, I say by its square.
- I name any square's colour within 2 seconds, with no board. Every bishop move in a blindfold game asks it.
- I name the squares between two squares on one diagonal, with no board, or know they share none. Every check, pin and block along a diagonal asks it.

Unproven: that these drills make my blindfold play better. No study found tests coordinate drills (my references, `drafts-and-science/what-it-means.md`).

## What it needs first

Nothing. The course starts here.

## The sections

**1.1 — The squares.** Where each square sits, and what it is called. 1.1.1 Find the square. 1.1.2 Name the square.

**1.2 — Square colours.** Each square's colour, from its name alone. 1.2.1 Name the colour.

**1.3 — Diagonals.** The squares a diagonal runs through. 1.3.1 Name the squares between.

**From my outline's five draft sections** (my handoff, `03-curriculum-outline.md`):

- Ranks and files — merged into 1.1. Every move names a whole square, and "Which file contains e4?" answers itself.
- Square identification and localization — kept, as 1.1.
- Light/dark-square recognition — kept, as 1.2.
- Adjacent squares, diagonals, and relative positions — split. Diagonals stay, as 1.3: one learner found the long-range pieces, queen and bishop, the hardest to track (`drafts-and-science/drafts/blog-marcus-folkesson-20-days-to-a-blindfold-game.md`). Neighbours and steps between squares go: they are how pieces move, not the board's own map.
- Mixed rapid mental navigation — merged into the path's last step, one mixed round. It trains nothing new, only the mix at speed.

## The challenges

### 1.1.1 — Find the square

**It trains.** Placing a named square on the board, at once.

**It needs.** Nothing.

**What I see.** An empty board: no pieces, nothing lit. I sit as White, with a1 at my bottom left. At L1, the letters a to h and the numbers 1 to 8 run along the board's edge. At the other levels, the board shows no names at all: it is only there to click on.

**The question.** "Find f6."

> **Stand-in.** Whether the square is also read aloud. I answer it later, with session 10. Every level plays in silence.

**How I answer.** I click one square.

**What counts as right.** The square named, on my one click. A click anywhere else is wrong, with no second try.

**Where its questions come from.** Any of the 64 squares, at random, none twice in a round, those coming back included. Never one on the file or the rank of the square before, so no question leans on the last. Squares coming back take places among them.

**Levels.**

| Level | Names on the edge | Time per question | Questions per round | Minutes per round |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| L1 | Yes | None | 20 | 3 |
| L2 | No | None | 20 | 3 |
| L3 | No | 2 seconds | 20 | 2 |

The names show first, then go: Chess.com's help advises beginners to keep them on at first (`existing-courses-and-drills/chesscom-vision-trainer.md`). Two seconds keeps placing a square from eating my thinking time in a game.

**Scoring.** One round counts my right answers out of 20, my average time per answer, and my mistakes by kind. At L3, no click within 2 seconds is a miss: too slow. A question left when the minutes run out is a miss.

> **Stand-in.** Pass mark for 1.1.1 L1 to L3: the right answers in one round, and my average time per answer. My practice sets it.

**After each answer.**

- Right: "Right: f6."
- Wrong, I clicked e6: "No: that is e6, the file beside. f6 is lit."
- Too slow: "Too slow. f6 is lit."

**Mistakes it names.** Each is a wrong square, one of my handoff's five kinds (`02-learning-principles.md`), told apart by the first of these it fits:

- The file beside: e6 for f6.
- The rank beside: f5 for f6.
- The board turned: c3 for f6. c3 sits in f6's place when I sit at the other side.
- The files reversed: c6 for f6. I counted the files from the wrong edge.
- The ranks reversed: f3 for f6. I counted the ranks from the wrong edge.
- Too slow: no click within 2 seconds, at L3.
- Another square: any other wrong one.

**What comes back.** A square I miss comes back once in each of my next two rounds holding this challenge. It follows my handoff's "revisit errors" (`02-learning-principles.md`).

**Examples.**

- **L1.** I sit as White, names on the edge. "Find g3." Right: I click g3. Wrong: I click g4, and I am told "No: that is g4, the rank beside. g3 is lit."
- **L2.** I sit as White, no names. "Find b6." Right: I click b6. Wrong: I click c6, and I am told "No: that is c6, the file beside. b6 is lit."
- **L3.** I sit as White, no names, 2 seconds. "Find e2." Right: I click e2 within 2 seconds. Wrong: no click within 2 seconds, and I am told "Too slow. e2 is lit."

### 1.1.2 — Name the square

**It trains.** Naming a square from its place, at once.

**It needs.** 1.1.1 L2.

**What I see.** An empty board: no pieces, no names on the edge, one square lit. I sit as White.

**The question.** "Which square is lit?"

**How I answer.** At L1, I pick one of four names: a block of 2×2 squares holding the lit square, such as b3, c3, b4 and c4, drawn at random among the blocks that hold it. Every choice sits next to the lit square, so only its exact name picks it. The four show in random order. From L2, I click the name on the square keypad: its file, a to h, then its rank, 1 to 8. Two clicks. The keypad offers every file and every rank, so it hints at nothing.

**What counts as right.** The lit square's name.

**Where its questions come from.** Any of the 64 squares, at random. Never one on the file or the rank of the square before. Squares coming back take places among them.

**Levels.**

| Level | How I answer | Time per question | Questions per round | Minutes per round |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| L1 | Pick one of four | None | 20 | 3 |
| L2 | Square keypad | None | 20 | 4 |
| L3 | Square keypad | 5 seconds | 20 | 3 |

Only L1 offers choices. From L2, I recall the name myself, file then rank: recalling an answer helped memory more than choosing one, in lab studies mostly on words (`drafts-and-science/science/rowland-2014-testing-effect-meta-analysis.md`). Five seconds leaves time for two clicks.

**Scoring.** One round counts my right answers out of 20, my average time per answer, and my mistakes by kind. At L3, no answer within 5 seconds is a miss: too slow. A question left when the minutes run out is a miss.

> **Stand-in.** Pass mark for 1.1.2 L1 to L3: the right answers in one round, and my average time per answer. My practice sets it.

**After each answer.**

- Right: "Right: b3."
- Wrong, I said c3: "No: it is b3. c3 is the file beside."
- Too slow: "Too slow. It is b3."

**Mistakes it names.** Each wrong name, by the first of these it fits:

- The file beside: c3 for b3.
- The rank beside: b4 for b3.
- The board turned: g6 for b3. g6 sits in b3's place when I sit at the other side.
- The files reversed: g3 for b3. I counted the files from the wrong edge.
- The ranks reversed: b6 for b3. I counted the ranks from the wrong edge.
- Too slow: no answer within 5 seconds, at L3.
- Another square: any other wrong one.

**What comes back.** A square I miss comes back once in each of my next two rounds holding this challenge.

**Examples.**

- **L1.** I sit as White, b3 lit. "Which square is lit?" The choices: b3, c3, b4, c4. Right: b3. Wrong: I pick c3, and I am told "No: it is b3. c3 is the file beside."
- **L2.** I sit as White, e5 lit. "Which square is lit?" Right: I click e, then 5. Wrong: I click e, then 6, and I am told "No: it is e5. e6 is the rank beside."
- **L3.** I sit as White, g2 lit, 5 seconds. "Which square is lit?" Right: I click g, then 2, within 5 seconds. Wrong: I click g, then 7, and I am told "No: it is g2. g7 is the ranks reversed."

### 1.2.1 — Name the colour

**It trains.** Knowing a square's colour from its name alone.

**It needs.** Nothing.

**What I see.** No board. Only the question.

**The question.** "What colour is f5?"

> **Stand-in.** Whether the square is also read aloud. I answer it later, with session 10. Every level plays in silence.

**How I answer.** I pick Light or Dark.

**What counts as right.** The square's colour on a real board. a1 is dark, and the colours alternate along every rank and file. The check: number the files a = 1 to h = 8, and add the rank. Even is dark, odd is light. f5 is 6 + 5 = 11: light.

**Where its questions come from.** Any of the 64 squares, at random. Never one on the file or the rank of the square before. Squares coming back take places among them.

**Levels.**

| Level | Time per question | Questions per round | Minutes per round |
|---|---|---|---|
| L1 | None | 20 | 3 |
| L2 | 2 seconds | 20 | 2 |

L2's two seconds asks me to know the colour, not add it up. One learner who added colours up later called it a mistake: he should have pictured the board (`drafts-and-science/drafts/blog-marcus-folkesson-20-days-to-a-blindfold-game.md`). One person's account.

**Scoring.** One round counts my right answers out of 20, my average time per answer, and my mistakes by kind. At L2, no pick within 2 seconds is a miss: too slow. A question left when the minutes run out is a miss.

> **Stand-in.** Pass mark for 1.2.1 L1 and L2: the right answers in one round, and my average time per answer. My practice sets it.

**After each answer.**

- Right: "Right: f5 is light."
- Wrong: "No: f5 is light. f is file 6, and 6 + 5 = 11 is odd."
- Too slow: "Too slow. f5 is light: 6 + 5 = 11 is odd."

**Mistakes it names.**

- The wrong colour: Dark for f5.
- Too slow: no pick within 2 seconds, at L2.

**What comes back.** A square I miss comes back once in each of my next two rounds holding this challenge.

**Examples.**

- **L1.** No board. "What colour is f5?" Right: Light. Wrong: I pick Dark, and I am told "No: f5 is light. f is file 6, and 6 + 5 = 11 is odd."
- **L2.** No board, 2 seconds. "What colour is c7?" Right: Dark, within 2 seconds. Wrong: I pick Light, and I am told "No: c7 is dark. c is file 3, and 3 + 7 = 10 is even."

### 1.3.1 — Name the squares between

**It trains.** Seeing a diagonal whole: the squares it runs through between two squares.

**It needs.** 1.1.1 L2.

**What I see.** At L1, an empty board, no names on the edge, no pieces, with the two squares lit. At L2, the same board with nothing lit. At L3, no board: only the question, a "No diagonal" button and a "Reveal board" button. Reveal shows the same board, nothing lit, to click. Where a board shows, I sit as White.

**The question.** "Which squares lie between c4 and f7 on their diagonal?"

> **Stand-in.** Whether the squares are also read aloud. I answer it later, with session 10. Every level plays in silence.

**How I answer.** Every answer is a click: no typing and no dictation. At L1 and L2, I click each square between, then confirm. At L3, I press "Reveal board", click each square on the empty board, then confirm. At every level, some pairs share no diagonal: I press "No diagonal", which needs no reveal at L3.

**What counts as right.** Exactly the squares between: none missing, none extra. Their order never counts. For a pair on no shared diagonal, only "No diagonal" is right. No part credit: in a game, one square missed is a piece in the way I never saw.

**Where its questions come from.** Two squares on one diagonal, 2 to 7 steps apart, at random: 1 to 6 squares lie between. A pair on no diagonal comes in three kinds, a third each: one square moved one file off a diagonal (c4 and e7, from c4 and f7); one moved one rank off a diagonal (c4 and e7, from c4 and e6); two squares on one file or rank, 2 to 7 apart (c4 and c7). The first two share no file, rank or diagonal. 6 of every 20 questions are a pair on no diagonal, at every level, and the answer is always "No diagonal".

> **Stand-in.** The final share of pairs on no diagonal. 30% stays until I rule on it.

**Levels.**

| Level | What the board shows | Pairs on no diagonal | How I answer | Questions per round | Minutes per round |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| L1 | Both squares lit | 6 of 20 | Click | 20 | 6 |
| L2 | Nothing lit | 6 of 20 | Click | 20 | 8 |
| L3 | No board until I reveal it | 6 of 20 | Click | 20 | 10 |

Twenty questions. No limit per question: a diagonal with six squares between takes longer than one with one.

**Scoring.** One round counts my right answers out of 20, my average time per answer, and my mistakes by kind. A question left when the minutes run out is a miss.

> **Stand-in.** Pass mark for 1.3.1 L1 to L3: the right answers in one round. My practice sets it.

**After each answer.** The board then lights the squares: a pair on a diagonal and the squares between, a pair on no diagonal just its two squares. At L3 it shows, revealed or not.

- Right: "Right: d5 and e6."
- Wrong, I gave d5 alone: "No: between c4 and f7 lie d5 and e6. You left out e6."
- Wrong, on a pair with no diagonal, the line says why by counting: "No: c4 and e7 share no diagonal. They sit 2 files and 3 ranks apart; a diagonal needs both equal."
- Wrong, on two squares on one file or rank: "No: c4 and c7 share a file, not a diagonal."

**Mistakes it names.** One answer can show two kinds at once.

- A square left out: d5 alone, for c4 and f7.
- A square off the diagonal: d6, given between c4 and f7.
- A diagonal that is not there: squares given for c4 and e7, which share none.
- A diagonal missed: "No diagonal", for c4 and f7.

**What comes back.** A pair I miss comes back once in each of my next two rounds holding this challenge.

**Examples.**

- **L1.** I sit as White, c4 and f7 lit. "Which squares lie between c4 and f7 on their diagonal?" Right: I click d5 and e6, then confirm. Wrong: I click d5 alone, and I am told "No: between c4 and f7 lie d5 and e6. You left out e6."
- **L1, on no diagonal.** I sit as White, c4 and e7 lit. "Which squares lie between c4 and e7 on their diagonal?" Right: I press "No diagonal". Wrong: I click d5, e6 and f7, and I am told "No: c4 and e7 share no diagonal. They sit 2 files and 3 ranks apart; a diagonal needs both equal."
- **L2.** I sit as White, nothing lit. "Which squares lie between h3 and e6 on their diagonal?" Right: I click g4 and f5. Wrong: I click g4 and f6, and I am told "No: between h3 and e6 lie g4 and f5. You left out f5, and f6 is off the diagonal."
- **L2, on one file.** I sit as White, nothing lit. "Which squares lie between c4 and c7 on their diagonal?" Right: I press "No diagonal". Wrong: I click c5, and I am told "No: c4 and c7 share a file, not a diagonal."
- **L3.** No board, only the question, "No diagonal" and "Reveal board". "Which squares lie between a1 and e5 on their diagonal?" Right: I press "Reveal board", click b2, c3 and d4, then confirm. Wrong: I reveal the board and click b2 and c3, and I am told "No: between a1 and e5 lie b2, c3 and d4. You left out d4."

## The path

Each step is one level, in this order.

1. 1.1.1 L1 — Find the square, names on the edge.
2. 1.1.1 L2 — Find the square, no names.
3. 1.2.1 L1 — Name the colour.
4. 1.1.2 L1 — Name the square, from four choices.
5. 1.1.2 L2 — Name the square, on the keypad.
6. 1.2.1 L2 — Name the colour, 2 seconds.
7. 1.3.1 L1 — Name the squares between, both lit.
8. 1.1.2 L3 — Name the square, 5 seconds.
9. 1.3.1 L2 — Name the squares between, nothing lit.
10. 1.1.1 L3 — Find the square, 2 seconds.
11. 1.3.1 L3 — Name the squares between, no board.
12. The mixed round.

The order climbs in tiers: every challenge's early levels come before any challenge's last one. The four skills grow together, as the mixed round asks.

**Step 12, the mixed round.** 23 questions within 5 minutes, every level at least once, the extra questions on each challenge's later levels. All 23 are shuffled together.

```
Challenge                   Questions   From each level
1.1.1 Find the square       4           L1 1, L2 1, L3 2
1.1.2 Name the square       4           L1 1, L2 1, L3 2
1.2.1 Name the colour       7           L1 3, L2 4
1.3.1 Name the squares between  8       L1 2, L2 3, L3 3
```

Each question is asked, timed and judged as at its own level, and keeps that level's names on the edge and time limit. The round counts my right answers out of 23, and the right answers in each challenge's share. It asks these as a blindfold game does: mixed, with no warning of which comes next.

> **Stand-in.** Pass mark for the mixed round: the right answers in one round, and in each challenge's share. My practice sets it.

Reaching it ends the chapter.
