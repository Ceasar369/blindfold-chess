# Chapter 6 — Blind play

My own moves, chosen with no board: the win a position holds, an endgame played to its end, and a whole game against a bot.

A level is one step of a challenge's difficulty: it sets each dial, such as how the position reaches me or how many hints a game gives, to an exact value.

A round is one sitting at one level: a fixed number of questions, ending on my score, or sooner when its minutes run out. In 6.3, a round is one game, and ends when the game does.

## What I can do at the end

- I find the winning move, a mate in one, a mate in two or a fork, in a real puzzle position of up to 7 pieces besides the kings, read once in words, after my opponent's move shown once. Every move my opponent makes in a blindfold game may leave a win, and I must see it with no board.
- I mate with a king and a queen, or a king and a rook, against a lone king that defends as well as it can, from the position read once, without stalemate. A won blindfold game ends this way.
- I take a king and a pawn against a lone king to a queen, from a position read once, where the position is won. A blindfold endgame often comes down to one pawn.
- I play a whole game against a bot from the starting position, each move shown once, with no hint and no illegal move, as White or as Black. That is my goal's first half: a full blindfold game against a bot.

Unproven: that these drills make my blindfold play better. The one study that trained blind tactics is weak: 27 students, twelve 2-hour lessons, its abstract alone read, "it cannot carry a design decision" (my references, `drafts-and-science/science/marchesich-tamburini-2024-blindfold-tactics-training.md`). Nothing tests a ladder of blind games (`drafts-and-science/what-it-means.md`). Players report from 20 days to about a year before a first full blind game: self-reports, all of them (same file).

- 4.2.1 L4 — a small position read in words, then hidden. Every endgame here starts so from 6.2.1 L3.

## The sections

**6.1 — Tactics.** The move that wins, found in a position I hold. 6.1.1 Find the winning move.

**6.2 — Endgames.** A small won endgame, played to its end against the best defence. 6.2.1 Play out the endgame.

**6.3 — Games.** A game against a bot, from a small real position, then from the start. 6.3.1 Play the game.

**Choosing my moves.** Every earlier page hands me the moves. Chapter 5 leaves choosing them, and finding what they win, to a later page. This is that page.

**Three answers.** A tactic asks one move, or two, and only the puzzle's are right, or any mate where it mates. An endgame asks a run of moves against a king that defends perfectly, and many runs are right: any that reaches the goal in time. A game asks moves to its end against a bot that tries to win, and nothing is right in advance: the result and my slips are its score. So they are three challenges. More pieces, a longer game, fewer hints or a stronger opponent change none of these answers: they are levels of 6.3.1, not challenges of their own.

**A move I write.** I build it on the move composer, key by key, as it is written: K, Q, R, B, N, the files, the ranks, x, =, + and #, and both castlings. Then Confirm. Every key is always on, so the keys hint at nothing. Each question starts the composer empty, a move refused too. Its signs, x, + and #, are passed over: the question is which move, and FIDE makes the signs optional (C.13, `notation-lookups/fide-laws-of-chess-notation.md`). 3.2.1 already judged them. A move naming no move, such as "N" alone, is not counted, and I am asked again: a slip is not a chess mistake (my handoff, `06-interaction-and-audio.md`). A move that leaves out what tells two of my pieces apart, or a promotion without its piece, is refused, and I am asked which: "Two of your rooks reach d1: say which." It counts as a move left unclear. The move I meant is still needed, so the question stays open.

**Moves shown.** As chapter 3 shows them, x, + and # included, numbered as the game numbers them: "28. Bxh6", "49... Rb2+". Where nothing renews the position, each move shows once, alone, as chapter 5 shows them. My own move shows back once, as the page read it, before the reply comes.

**A position in words.** As chapter 4 writes it, with whose move it is: "White: king f7, rook f3. Black: king h6, rook e4, bishop d4, pawns b6, f4, g5 and h7. White to move." No position given here in words allows castling or a pawn taken in passing: puzzles and game starts are picked so, and a set-up endgame allows neither. So a position in words needs nothing but its pieces and its side to move.

**Checkmate and stalemate.** Checkmate: the side to move is in check and has no legal move; it wins the game at once (FIDE 1.4 and 5.1.1). Stalemate: the side to move has no legal move and is not in check; it draws at once (FIDE 5.2.1). A dead position draws at once too: neither side can mate any longer, as king against king (FIDE 5.2.2) (`blind-play-lookups/fide-laws-of-chess-game-end.md`). Chapter 2 trained check only. Here mate is the answer of every mate puzzle in 6.1.1, the goal of 6.2.1's queen and rook endgames, and the end of a won game in 6.3.1. Stalemate is the named mistake of 6.2.1, and of 6.3.1 when I give it. A real case: in a Lichess game of January 2013, players rated 1462 and 1309, White had a queen, two rooks and five pawns against one pawn, and played 34. h4. Black's king on a4 had no move: stalemate, a draw. 34. Qb3# mated (`blind-play-lookups/lichess-games-2013-01-blind-play.md`).

**Castling.** Castling is allowed only when the king and that rook have never moved, no piece stands between them, the king is not in check, and it neither crosses nor lands on an attacked square (FIDE 3.8.2, `piece-movement-lookups/fide-laws-of-chess-moves.md`). Only a whole game holds a king's and a rook's history, so only 6.3.1's whole games ask it. Every other position here allows no castling. Castling I may not do is an illegal move, refused like any other. After the game, the page names the rule it broke: "5... O-O was not legal: your bishop on f8 stands between your king and your rook."

**Illegal moves.** In a puzzle, a move that is no move is wrong. In an endgame or a game, it is refused: "Not counted. The move stays open." It is not played, and it counts as an illegal move. I am asked again. The refusal never says why: the reason would show me the board. After the endgame or the game, the page says why. In a tournament, a second illegal move loses the game (FIDE 7.5.5, `blind-play-lookups/fide-laws-of-chess-game-end.md`). Not here: the game goes on, and the pass marks read my illegal moves. One player's first blind game held two illegal moves (`drafts-and-science/drafts/chesscom-blog-lpindar-my-first-real-blindfold-game.md`).

**Where I lost the thread.** As chapter 5 finds it: the last move that took my answer off a square. Here my answer is a move. When the piece I name stood earlier where my move would have worked, the move counted is the last one that took it off that square. "15... Nxd4 was not legal: your knight from c6 was taken on f3 at 13. Bxf3."

**Hints.** Only in 6.3.1, at the levels that hide the position. Each is a button under the move composer, showing its cost. It costs hint points from the game's budget. A hint costing more than my points left is off:

- **A square.** I pick a square on the keypad, its file, then its rank, as 1.1.2 asks it, and I am told what stands there, as 4.1.1 answers it: "Black's pawn", or "Empty". 1 point: it answers one square.
- **The last moves.** The last 6 half-moves show, numbered, until I hide them, as 5.1.1 L3's checkpoints come every 6. 1 point: it answers what changed. A half-move is one side's move.
- **The board.** Every piece shows on a board facing White, for 5 seconds. 3 points: it answers every square at once. Five seconds is a glance, not a study.

A game gives 6 points at its hint levels, then none at the last: two looks at the board, or six squares. Help that aids beginners can hurt stronger learners, and help fitted to each learner's progress beat a fixed schedule, in school and lab studies (`drafts-and-science/science/kalyuga-2007-expertise-reversal-review.md`, `drafts-and-science/science/salden-2010-expertise-reversal-worked-examples.md`). No rule for when to give help exists (`drafts-and-science/science/koedinger-aleven-2007-assistance-dilemma.md`). So the budget falls on a fixed ladder, and the pass marks read what I spend. Unproven for chess. Other trainers give the same kinds: a peek at the real position (`existing-courses-and-drills/fritz-chessbase-blindfold-play.md`), a token that shows hidden pieces (`existing-courses-and-drills/eyegambit.md`), hints early and fewer later (`existing-courses-and-drills/chess-com-jugadabrillante-learn-to-play-blindfold-chess.md`). One forum's ladder allows about 5 takebacks at first (`drafts-and-science/drafts/lichess-forum-how-to-play-blindfold-as-an-average-player.md`). Not here: a takeback undoes a chess mistake, not a lost board, and an illegal move is already refused.

**The opponent.** In 6.2.1, a lone king that defends perfectly: each move puts off the goal the longest, as the endgame tables give it. The endgame tables hold the result of every position of up to 7 pieces, worked out in advance (`content-and-progress/content/lichess-tablebase-api.md`). Where two moves put it off alike, either. In 6.3.1, a bot: it plays like a person of its strength, varies its openings, moves at once, never resigns, and offers no draw. It comes at two strengths: weaker than me, then even with me.

> **Stand-in.** The bots' strengths: the weaker about 200 points below my rating, the even one at my rating, near 1000. No bot found is checked at 1000: the one built to play like people starts at 1100, and its Lichess bot plays above that, and the strongest free chess program's weakest setting is about 1320 (`reuse-digest/by-chapter.md`, `chess-rules-and-bots/by-use.md`). Unproven that any bot plays like a person at either strength. My practice sets both.

**How a game ends.** Checkmate, stalemate and a dead position, as above. I may resign, with the Resign button. A position's third appearance, and 50 moves each with no capture and no pawn move, draw at once. In a tournament, a player claims those (FIDE 9.2 and 9.3), and only the fifth appearance or 75 moves draw unclaimed (FIDE 9.6). Here no one claims: blind, I cannot check a repetition or count 50 moves.

**The side I sit on.** In 6.1.1, the side to move in the puzzle's game, White or Black, as Lichess gives it: a game asks tactics of either side. In 6.2.1, the side with the queen, rook or pawn, White or Black at random each round, kept for the round. In 6.3.1, White first: White moves first, so the game opens on my own move, and one forum's advice is to start with White (`drafts-and-science/drafts/lichess-forum-blindfold-chess-where-to-start.md`). Then Black, then either at random each game, so the last level seats me as a game does. Another forum's ladder moves up only after a win with each colour (`drafts-and-science/drafts/lichess-forum-how-to-play-blindfold-as-an-average-player.md`). Where a board shows, it faces White whichever side I play.

**Moves read aloud.** Whether the app reads moves aloud waits for me: each card carries a stand-in at its question. Every level plays in silence.

**Playing people.** My goal's last step, after the bot. It is not a challenge: it happens outside the app. A stand-in after the path holds it.

**What this page leaves out.** A clock: no level times a game, and my goal against a bot needs none. Accuracy comes first (my handoff, `02-learning-principles.md`). Draw offers and takebacks. A whole position said back: chapters 4 and 5 ask it, so no answer here pairs pieces as 4.2.1 does. Choices in an endgame or a game: another trainer lists every legal move as a button (`github-trainers/pages/cgoldammer--chess-blindfold.md`), which names every square my pieces reach, the very thing I hold.

**From my outline's five draft sections** (my handoff, `03-curriculum-outline.md`):

- Simple mental tactics and threats — kept, as 6.1. Every puzzle opens on my opponent's move, so its win punishes what that move allowed. Threats against me come in 6.3's games, from the bot's moves. One study of 44,213 Chess.com players found puzzle and lesson time gave 3.61 times the rating gain of games, hour for hour: observational, and not blind (`drafts-and-science/science/southwick-2026-not-all-practice-equal.md`).
- Small endgames — kept, as 6.2. One player's ladder runs queen, rook, then pawn against a lone king, before any game (`existing-courses-and-drills/chess-com-leeeuler-learning-to-play-blindfold-chess.md`).
- Simplified positions against an opponent — merged into 6.3.1's first levels: a game from a real position of 6 pieces besides the kings. Fewer pieces is a level, not a challenge.
- Complete games with optional checks/hints — merged into 6.3.1's middle levels: whole games with 6 hint points.
- Complete blindfold games without board assistance — merged into 6.3.1's last level, the goal. Drafts agree full games come last, and that a full game too early is the most common reported failure (`drafts-and-science/what-it-means.md`).

## The challenges

### 6.1.1 — Find the winning move

**It trains.** Finding the move that wins, in a position held in my head.

**It needs.** 1.1.1 L2, 2.3.1 L3, 2.3.2 L3 and 3.1.1 L4. From L2, 3.2.1 L4. From L4, 2.2.1 L4 and 3.2.1 L5. From L5, 4.1.1 L5 and 5.1.1 L2.

**What I see.** At L1 to L3, a board with no names on its edge, every piece, as the position stands after my opponent's last move. That move's two squares are lit. The board faces White, and I play the side to move. At L4, no board: the same position in words, which stays on screen. At L5, no board: the position before my opponent's last move, in words, until I hide them. Then my opponent's move alone. Nothing shows the position. In a two-move puzzle, my opponent's reply comes after my first move: on the board at L1 to L3, in the renewed words at L4, alone at L5.

**The question.** "White played 28. Bxh6. Find your winning move." In a two-move puzzle, after my first move: "Black played 33... Ne8. Your next move."

> **Stand-in.** Whether the question, and at L4 and L5 the position in words, is also read aloud. I answer it later, with session 10. Every level plays in silence.

**How I answer.** At L1, I pick one of four moves: the win, and three other moves of mine, checks first, then captures, then any, at random among them, never another move that mates. From L2, I build my move on the move composer, then Confirm.

**What counts as right.** The puzzle's move, signs passed over: "Rh3" counts for 51. Rh3#. Where the puzzle's move mates, any move that mates counts: a mate is a mate. Lichess counts every mate in one as solving its mate-in-one puzzles (`content-and-progress/content/lichess-puzzle-database.md`). In a two-move puzzle, both my moves must be right. My opponent's reply between them is the one the puzzle gives. A wrong first move ends the puzzle. Any other move, legal or not, is wrong. No part credit: a tactic begun and not finished wins nothing.

**Where its questions come from.** Real puzzles from Lichess's puzzle file: each stood in a game people played, and names that game (`content-and-progress/content/lichess-puzzle-database.md`). All rated under 1000. A puzzle's rating is Lichess's, not a player's (same file). One forum's advice is to take puzzles well below my level (`drafts-and-science/drafts/chesscom-forum-can-the-average-person-learn-blindfold-page4.md`). A puzzle that still allows castling or a pawn taken in passing is left out: words never show either.

- **Mate in one:** Lichess's theme mateIn1.
- **Two moves:** a mate in two, mate on my second move whatever the reply; or a fork in two moves, a move that attacks two pieces, then the capture. Lichess's themes mateIn2, and fork with two moves of mine.
- **At L4 and L5:** at most 7 pieces besides the kings, before my opponent's move, as chapter 4's words hold.

In the file's first 599,512 puzzles, under 1000 and allowing neither castling nor a pawn taken in passing: 44,896 mates in one, 32,137 mates in two and 15,193 two-move forks. Of 7 or fewer besides the kings: 1,572, 923 and 1,461 (`blind-play-lookups/lichess-puzzles-first-slice.md`). One move that takes a loose piece with no mate is rare there: 28, none of 7 or fewer. So mates and forks are the tactics here.

Puzzles coming back take places among them.

Two moves join at L3, on a board: one forum raises the moves a puzzle asks over time (`drafts-and-science/drafts/chesscom-clubs-forum-blindfold-chess-threads.md`), another starts with few pieces (`drafts-and-science/drafts/lichess-forum-how-to-improve-blindfold-chess.md`). **Scoring.** One round counts my puzzles right out of 10 or 6, my two-move puzzles' first moves right, my average time per puzzle, and my mistakes by kind. A question left when the minutes run out is a miss.

> **Stand-in.** Pass mark for 6.1.1 L1 to L5: the right answers in one round. My practice sets it.

**After each answer.** The puzzle's moves show on a board. Each wrong answer says why: explanations helped more than "wrong" alone, in computer lessons at school and college (`drafts-and-science/science/van-der-kleij-2015-feedback-computer-meta-analysis.md`).

- Right: "Right: 51. Rh3#."
- Right, two moves: "Right: 33. Rd8+ Ne8 34. Rxe8#."
- Wrong, a check that is not mate: "No: it is 28... Nxg3#. Rh2+ is check, but White's king takes the rook on h2."
- Wrong, a square one off: "No: it is 51. Rh3#. g3 is the file beside h3."
- Wrong, a move from before my opponent's: "No: your pawn on c2 was taken by 49. Rxc2. It is 49... Ne1+, then 50... Nxc2: the knight checks the king, then takes the rook."

**Mistakes it names.** A wrong move takes the first kind it fits.

- Chapter 1's five, on the square my move lands on, when it moves the right piece. With 51. Rh3# right, in 00OXc: Rg3, the file beside. Rh2 or Rh4, the rank beside. Ra6, the board turned. Ra3, the files reversed. Rh6, the ranks reversed.
- A move from before my opponent's move: it was a move before that move, and is none after it. c1=Q, in 01FzV: 49. Rxc2 took the pawn. A sequence lost, one of my handoff's five kinds (`02-learning-principles.md`).
- A move that is no move: the piece cannot go there. Rf6, in 00OXc: Black's pawn on f4 stands between f3 and f6. A move read wrongly, one of my handoff's five.
- A check that is not mate: Rh2+, in 002Q2. White's king takes the rook.
- Another move: any other legal move. Qxg4, in 0030b: it wins a rook, and Black plays on, where 33. Rd8+ mates in two.

**What comes back.** A puzzle I miss comes back once in each of my next two rounds holding this challenge, mirrored: each piece keeps its rank and moves to the other wing, a to h, as chapter 4 mirrors a position. The same tactic comes back on new squares. The same puzzle would train its answer, remembered. No puzzle here allows castling, so every mirror is legal. 00OXc, mirrored: White: king c7, rook c3. Black: king a6, rook d4, bishop e4, pawns a7, b5, c4 and g6, after 50... b5. The answer: 51. Ra3# (`blind-play-lookups/lichess-puzzles-first-slice.md`).

### 6.2.1 — Play out the endgame

**It trains.** Winning a small endgame against the best defence, with the board in my head.

**It needs.** 1.1.1 L2, 2.3.2 L3, 3.2.1 L4 and 6.1.1 L2. From L2, 3.1.1 L6 and 3.2.1 L5. From L3, 4.2.1 L4 and 5.1.1 L2.

**What I see.** At L1, a board with no names on its edge and the pieces, renewed after each move. The board faces White, and I play my side. At L2 and L4, no board: the position in words, renewed after each move, as at 3.1.1 L6. At L3, L5 and L6, no board: the position in words until I hide them. Then each of the defending king's moves alone, until I make mine. Nothing shows the position.

**The question.** "Mate Black's king, in 14 moves at most." With the pawn: "Make your pawn a queen, in 10 moves at most."

> **Stand-in.** Whether the moves, and the position in words, are also read aloud. I answer it later, with session 10. Every level plays in silence.

**How I answer.** I build each of my moves on the move composer, then Confirm. The defending king replies after each. The endgame runs until the goal, a draw, or my last move allowed.

**What counts as right.** The goal, within the moves allowed: twice the fewest my side needs from the start, as the endgame tables count them. With a queen or a rook, the goal is mate. With the pawn, it is a queen that Black cannot take at once, with Black not stalemated: a queen whose mate L1 to L3 trained. Any route counts. It is wrong, and stops at once, when my move throws the win away: a stalemate; a piece left where the king takes it, king against king, a dead position; or, with the pawn, a move after which the tables say draw. It is wrong when my moves run out. An illegal move is refused, counted, and uses up no move. No part credit: a win thrown away at the last move is a draw.

**Where its questions come from.** Positions set up for the drill, as chapter 2 sets them up: both kings, never side by side, the side not to move never in check, no pawn on the first or eighth rank. My side is to move. Every such position with a queen or a rook is a win, in 10 moves at most with the queen and 16 with the rook (Wikipedia, Checkmate, citing Fine and Benko; my own count of all 144,508 and 175,168 positions agrees, `blind-play-lookups/basic-endgames.md`). With the pawn, only won positions are asked: 124,954 of 163,328.
